## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A student building a career brand produces the following brand strategy draft:

> - **Archetype:** Sage
> - **Mission:** Help data teams work smarter
> - **Vision:** A world where data is accessible to everyone
> - **UVP:** I build data pipelines that are fast and reliable
> - **Positioning:** For data teams who need better tools
> - **Voice:** Professional and friendly
> - **Negative space:** [blank]

**Task:** Identify the weakest component, explain why it is weak using chapter vocabulary, and rewrite it to function properly.

**Model Answer:**

The weakest component is the **negative space** — it is blank. The chapter identifies negative space as the most structurally important component of a brand strategy, because it defines what the brand is *not*, which is what makes the brand's claims credible and its positioning defensible.

A blank negative space means the strategy makes no exclusion decisions. The UVP ("fast and reliable pipelines") and positioning ("data teams who need better tools") apply to essentially every data engineer alive. The brand has no edges.

**Revised negative space:**
> I do not build general-purpose dashboards, business intelligence layers, or reporting tools. I do not work on tools where the primary user is a non-technical executive. I do not take projects that are primarily data visualization rather than pipeline architecture.

This negative space performs the chapter's function: it makes the positioning claim ("developer-facing pipeline infrastructure for technical data teams") credible by defining what would be declined. The brand now has a visible edge.

**Secondary weakness:** "Mission: Help data teams work smarter" is a non-falsifiable aspiration, not a mission statement. The chapter specifies that a mission is a *present-tense* claim about the work you do, not an aspiration about outcomes. Revision: "I build production-grade data pipelines for technical teams who need infrastructure they can trust."

## Answer Key

### Tier 1

**Exercise 1 — Component Classification**

All seven statements are assigned to the correct components. The misassigned analysis:

**Statement B ("Transparency is how I build") → assigned to Voice, but this is a Values statement.** Voice governs *how* the brand speaks; Values govern *what the brand believes* guides its work. "Transparency" is a value; a voice expression would be "I explain design decisions in writing before I write code."

**Statement E ("I speak directly, use precise technical vocabulary") → assigned to Values, but this is a Voice statement.** This describes how the brand communicates (directly, technically), which is voice. A values statement would be "I believe technical precision is a form of respect for the people I work with."

**Poorly written component:** Statement D — "There is no one else building AI-native competitive intelligence tools specifically for seed-stage SaaS companies." This is labeled UVP but is written as a market observation, not a claim about the individual. A UVP is a first-person statement: "I am the only AI engineer building competitive intelligence infrastructure specifically for seed-stage SaaS companies — because I have run pipeline for three of them."

**Chapter reference:** §1, "Seven Components."
**Common error:** Confusing voice (how you communicate) with values (what guides your work). They overlap in expression but are structurally distinct.

---

**Exercise 2 — Negative Space**

a) "I build AI agents that replace manual research workflows."
> Negative space: I do not build search tools, recommendation engines, or creative content generators. I do not work on tools where the primary output is a document rather than an action. I do not take projects where the "AI" is primarily a text formatter rather than a reasoning agent.

b) "I design AI interfaces for users with limited technical background."
> Negative space: I do not design developer tools, API interfaces, or command-line products. I do not work on B2B enterprise software where the primary user is a technical team. I do not build interfaces that require users to understand prompts, tokens, or model architecture.

c) "I develop production ML models for clinical decision support."
> Negative space: I do not build consumer health apps, wellness trackers, or chatbots for patient engagement. I do not work on predictive models where clinical validation is not part of the scope. I do not take projects where the primary user is a patient rather than a clinician.

**Chapter reference:** §2, "Negative Space."
**Common error:** Writing negative space as "I don't use X technology" rather than "I don't serve X audience / build X type of thing." Negative space is about scope, audience, and use case, not about technology preferences.

---

**Exercise 3 — Stripe Case Study**

a) Stripe primarily expresses the **Sage archetype** — the brand's defining commitment is to the intelligence and capability of its developers, not to transcendence (Magician), belonging (Everyman), or authority (Ruler). Evidence from the chapter: Stripe's documentation is the primary brand artifact, written for developers who want to understand the mechanism, not just use the API.

b) A tone choice is adjustable — a brand can be "more formal" or "less formal" depending on context. A structural commitment governs what the brand will and will not do across *all* contexts. Stripe's developer-first voice is structural because the brand has organized its entire product philosophy, documentation investment, and hiring around serving developers — it cannot be "turned off" without ceasing to be Stripe.

c) Negative space restricts where the brand will compete, which makes the remaining claims credible because the brand cannot be accused of overpromising. Stripe's "not a consumer product" exclusion means that everything Stripe builds is optimized for developers and businesses — no dilution toward consumer UX priorities. The restriction is what makes the developer-first claim trustworthy: the brand has given up a market to focus on this one.

**Chapter reference:** §3, "Stripe as Primary Case."

| a) "NeuralNexus" | Fail — hard to say/remember clearly in noise | Pass — legally distinctive | Likely taken | Fail |
| b) "Priya Builds" | Pass — clear, memorable, speakable | Pass — personal name + verb, distinctive | Pass — likely available as domain | Pass |

**Lawyer test:** Is the name legally distinctive (not purely descriptive, not trademarked)?

**Chapter reference:** §4, "Three Naming Tests."
**Common error:** Treating the domain test as optional. The chapter specifies it as a hard requirement — a name without an ownable domain cannot anchor a digital professional presence.

**Exercise 5 — Internal Consistency Check**

**Inconsistency 1:** Archetype (Outlaw) vs. Positioning (enterprise compliance teams). Outlaw archetypes derive energy from challenging authority and convention. Enterprise compliance teams *are* the authority — they represent regulatory constraints. An Outlaw brand cannot credibly serve the function that enforces the rules it challenges. The tension is irresolvable without changing either the archetype or the positioning.

**Inconsistency 2:** Voice ("provocative, anti-conventional") vs. Values ("rigor, precision, transparency, accountability"). Rigor, precision, and accountability are Sage or Ruler values. They fit a brand that emphasizes expertise and reliability. An Outlaw's values would be autonomy, disruption, and non-conformity. The values list and the voice are from two different archetypes.

**Inconsistency 3:** Mission ("build AI systems responsibly") vs. Archetype (Outlaw). "Responsibly" is a Caregiver/Sage qualifier. The Outlaw archetype builds things that challenge the current order, not things that comply with it. The mission would be coherent for a Sage or Caregiver; it contradicts an Outlaw's core motivation.

**Resolution:** Either change the archetype to Sage (which makes all components cohere) or rewrite the mission, voice, and values to express the Outlaw archetype authentically (e.g., mission: "I build AI systems that expose what compliance frameworks miss").

---

**Exercise 6 — One-Page Constraint**

a) The one-page constraint is a forcing function because it requires the author to make trade-off decisions — choosing which formulations are essential and which are elaborations that can be cut. A strategy that cannot fit on one page has not yet resolved all its trade-offs; it is still in the thinking stage, not the deciding stage.

b) The revision discipline required: cut all rationale, all examples, and all "because" clauses. Each component is expressed as a single sentence or a short list. If any component requires explanation to be understood, it is not yet precise enough to be a strategic commitment.

c) A brand strategy that cannot be condensed to one page reveals that the strategy has not yet achieved specificity. Long strategies contain internally inconsistent components (which require extensive caveats), generic components (which require multiple hedges to avoid overclaiming), or unresolved trade-offs (which require "it depends" qualifications). Precision eliminates bulk.

---

**Exercise 7 — Helen Lansdowne Resor**

(a) Helen Lansdowne Resor was the first major female copywriter and creative director in American advertising (at J. Walter Thompson), who pioneered the strategy of positioning products to specific audiences by building a complete picture of who the customer was and what they valued — the origin of audience-centered brand strategy.

(b) The chapter derives from this the principle that brand strategy is fundamentally about *who the brand is for* and *what it refuses to be* — that the discipline of Resor's copywriting (knowing the audience specifically enough to write for them and not for everyone) is the same discipline the chapter asks students to apply to their own personal brand.

---

**Exercise 8 — Component Hierarchy**

**Ranking (most to least structurally important, by chapter logic):**

1. **Negative space** — The chapter explicitly names this as most important; it makes all other claims credible
2. **Archetype** — The archetype governs voice, positioning, and story shape; without it, all other components drift
3. **Positioning** — Defines the competitive context; the claim on which UVP and mission depend
4. **UVP** — The specific first-person claim that makes positioning concrete
5. **Mission** — Anchors the brand in present-tense action; enables consistency check
6. **Voice** — Expresses archetype in language; downstream of archetype and positioning
7. **Vision** — Long-range aspiration; least constraining of daily decisions

**Top-3 justifications:**
- Negative space: Without edges, every other claim is unverifiable.
- Archetype: The archetype determines what story shapes, voice registers, and audience types are appropriate — it is the filter on everything else.
- Positioning: Without specifying the audience and competitive context, UVP and mission cannot be evaluated.

**Bottom-2 justifications:**
- Voice: Important for execution but derived from archetype; a correct archetype produces correct voice.
- Vision: Long-range statements are aspirational and rarely constrain short-term decisions; they contribute brand coherence but are the least operationally load-bearing.

**Exercise 10 — Error Analysis**

**Inconsistency 1 — Archetype (Magician) vs. Voice/Values (Sage).**
"Authoritative, precise, technical" (voice) and "accuracy, rigor, peer-reviewed methodology" (values) are Sage-register. The Magician's voice is visionary, transformative, and mystery-oriented — not technical precision. Fix: Either change archetype to Sage, or rewrite voice to "transformative, possibility-forward, reveals the non-obvious" and values to "transformation, vision, radical rethinking."

**Inconsistency 2 — UVP vs. all other components.**
"I am the most technically rigorous AI engineer applying for this role" is a competitive UVP for a job application — it references other applicants ("for this role"). A UVP in a brand strategy document is a claim about why the specific audience should choose this brand over all alternatives, not a comparison to other job candidates. Fix: "I build AI tools that transform how marketing teams understand their customers at a level of mechanism depth that strategy consultants cannot provide."

**Inconsistency 3 — Positioning ("behind on AI adoption") vs. Archetype (Magician).**
The Magician archetype serves customers who are ready to be transformed — who believe the impossible can become possible. A positioning statement aimed at teams "behind on AI adoption" describes laggards, not visionaries. Magician storytelling requires an audience with enough readiness to engage with transformation. Fix: "For marketing teams at the forward edge of AI adoption who need to translate experimental models into production intelligence."

---

**Exercise 11 — Positioning vs. UVP**

Positioning specifies *who* the brand serves and *where* it sits in the competitive landscape. UVP specifies *why* the brand should be chosen over alternatives — the first-person claim of distinctive value.

For Stripe: **Positioning** would be "For developers and businesses who need payment infrastructure that works reliably at scale." This names the audience (developers and businesses at scale) and the context (payment infrastructure). **UVP** would be "Stripe is the only payment platform built by developers for developers — where the primary product is the quality of the API, not the payment processing itself." This is the first-person claim of what makes Stripe specifically preferable to PayPal or Braintree: the API-quality commitment, not just the functionality.

They are distinct because positioning could be shared by a competitor (PayPal also serves developers building at scale), but UVP cannot — the API-first design philosophy is specific to Stripe's strategic choices.

## Instructor Notes

**Prerequisite dependencies:** Chapter 3 (archetype) is required for Exercises 1, 5, 9, 10, 14, 15, 16, 18, and 19. Students without an archetype commitment will write generic strategy documents. Exercise 12 requires Chapter 7 interface layer vocabulary.

**Highest-yield exercises for class discussion:** Exercise 6 (one-page constraint as forcing function) and Exercise 5 (internal consistency check for Outlaw/compliance tension) generate the richest discussion. The Outlaw/compliance inconsistency in Exercise 5 often resonates with students who have tried to position themselves as "responsible AI" engineers while also wanting to signal that they challenge convention.

**Common student error:** Writing a brand strategy where every component is aspirational ("vision: a world where AI is ethical") rather than operational ("mission: I build AI tools for X audience that do Y thing"). The one-page constraint exercise is the best corrective — aspiration consumes space that precision doesn't need.

**Tier 4 assessment note:** Exemplary responses will demonstrate that the negative space is the most specific and hardest-fought component — students who write rich, credible negative spaces have genuinely internalized the chapter's central argument. Generic negative spaces ("I don't do things that harm people") reveal that the student has not yet made the positioning trade-offs.
