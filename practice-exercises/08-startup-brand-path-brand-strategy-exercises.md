## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A student is building a startup brand for an AI-powered legal contract review tool. They produce the following brand strategy draft:

> - **Company Name:** LegalEase
> - **Archetype:** Sage
> - **Mission:** Make legal review faster
> - **Vision:** A world where legal work doesn't slow down business
> - **UVP:** We review contracts faster than any human lawyer
> - **Positioning:** For startups who need legal review
> - **Voice:** Professional
> - **Negative space:** [blank — "We'll figure this out later"]

**Task:** Identify the three most significant problems with this draft, using chapter vocabulary. For each problem, name the chapter concept it violates and provide a revised version.

**Model Answer:**

**Problem 1 — No-list (negative space) is blank.**
The chapter specifies that the no-list is "the most operationally useful component of a startup brand strategy" because it prevents scope creep — the same force that destroys PRDs (Chapter 4) and pipelines (Chapter 5). A blank no-list means the strategy has no edges; every future feature request, partnership, or audience expansion will appear plausible. "We'll figure this out later" is a guarantee that the brand will drift.

**Revision:** "We are not a contract drafting tool. We are not a general legal research assistant. We are not built for law firms or legal teams — our user is the non-lawyer founder. We do not replace lawyers; we surface what needs a lawyer's attention."

**Problem 2 — UVP is borrowed from competitors.**
"Faster than any human lawyer" is a speed claim that any contract review tool could make. The chapter specifies that a UVP must be *specific* to this brand — the claim that makes your tool non-interchangeable with the alternatives. Speed alone is not a UVP; it is a feature.

**Revision:** "LegalEase is the only contract review tool built for non-lawyer founders — it surfaces the specific clauses that first-time founders consistently miss, not the ones lawyers optimize for."

**Problem 3 — Positioning is too broad.**
"For startups who need legal review" is everyone. The chapter specifies that positioning defines the *specific* audience segment and the *specific* competitive context. Without specificity, the brand cannot be coherent — its design, voice, and features will be pulled in all directions.

**Revision:** "For technical founders at pre-Series A startups who are reviewing their first vendor agreements, employment contracts, and customer terms of service without in-house legal counsel."

## Answer Key

### Tier 1

**Exercise 1 — Component Classification**

All eight components are correctly assigned. The incoherent statement:

**Statement G — "We will transform how marketing teams worldwide understand their competition."**
This is assigned as Negative space, which is incorrect — it is a vision statement (aspirational, long-range, global scale). But even moved to Vision, it is incoherent with the **Everyman archetype**. The Everyman promises belonging and accessibility — "for everyone, as they are." "Transform how marketing teams worldwide" is Magician language (transformation at scale). The Everyman vision should be democratic and proximate: "A world where small marketing teams have the same competitive intelligence that enterprise teams have."

**Chapter reference:** §1, "Eight Components" and §2, "Archetype Filter."
**Common error:** Treating "vision" and "transformation" as universally compatible. Vision statements should be archetype-filtered just like all other components.

---

**Exercise 2 — No-List**

a) The no-list is more operationally critical for a startup because a startup must make daily decisions about features, partnerships, and customer requests — each of which creates pressure to expand scope. The no-list is designed to resist **scope creep**, the compounding force the chapter identifies (borrowing from Chapter 4's PRD) as the primary cause of startup brand dilution. A personal brand faces the same pressure but at lower frequency and lower stakes; a startup faces it at every investor meeting, every sales call, and every customer feature request.

b) Sample no-list for biometric workout planner:
> 1. We are not a nutrition tracking tool.
> 2. We are not a social fitness app — no sharing, leaderboards, or community features.
> 3. We are not a general health monitoring platform.
> 4. We do not integrate with medical devices or clinical-grade monitors.
> 5. We do not offer workout coaching by human trainers.

c) A no-list with one item reveals that the startup has not yet confronted its actual scope trade-offs. One exclusion is likely obvious (e.g., "we don't serve enterprise clients") and does not constrain anything the startup was actually tempted to build. A credible no-list contains items the startup was *actually* considering and chose to decline.

---

**Exercise 3 — Stripe by Inversion**

a) Three visible product/design decisions:
1. Stripe builds its documentation as a primary product — the docs are more carefully designed than most software
2. Stripe does not offer a consumer-facing payment app or wallet
3. Stripe's pricing is public, transparent, and per-transaction — no enterprise sales process required for standard tiers

b) Brand strategy components expressed:
1. Documentation as primary product → Archetype (Sage: the mechanism is the story); Voice (technical, precise, teaching-oriented)
2. No consumer product → No-list (commitment to developer/business audience only)
3. Public, transparent pricing → Values (transparency, developer respect); Positioning (accessible to solo developers and startups, not just enterprises)

c) The reverse-engineering method reveals the *decisions Stripe has refused* — which are often more revealing than what it has built. The refusal to build a consumer product, the refusal to add a visual editor for non-developers, the refusal to simplify documentation to marketing language — these absences reveal the strategic commitments more clearly than any brand language Stripe publishes.

**Exercise 7 — Bill Bernbach**

(a) Bill Bernbach co-founded DDB (Doyle Dane Bernbach) and pioneered the idea that advertising effectiveness came from creative specificity — saying one true, surprising thing about a product rather than making comprehensive feature claims. His work on the VW "Think Small" campaign (1959) demonstrated that honest, specific, anti-hype brand strategy could outperform bombastic positioning.

(b) The chapter derives from Bernbach the principle that startup brand strategy requires a single, specific, defensible claim — not a comprehensive description of everything the startup does. The no-list enforces this by making the brand commit to what it will not do, freeing the positive components to be genuinely specific rather than exhaustive.

---

**Exercise 8 — No-List as Scope Enforcement**

a) The no-list functions like an MVP Boundary because both define what is out of scope, and both resist the same force: the pressure to expand scope in response to external requests. The MVP Boundary (Ch. 4) prevents "the $100,000 no" — declining a feature that would pull the product off its defined scope. The no-list prevents the analogous brand expansion: declining a market segment, use case, or positioning claim that would dilute the brand's credibility.

b) **Amazon** (not an AI startup, but the most documented example of brand scope creep): Amazon's original no-list would have been "we sell books, not everything." Expanding beyond that no-list produced Marketplace, AWS, Prime, groceries, healthcare, and devices. The brand became incoherent as a consumer proposition (what is "Amazon"?), though financially successful. A startup example: **Slack** originally positioned as a developer team tool; expanded to enterprise; brand credibility with startup developers eroded as it became an enterprise product.

c) A startup should expand beyond its original no-list only when: (1) the expansion is demanded by existing customers (not new ones), (2) the expansion is consistent with the archetype and does not require a different voice, and (3) the expansion can be made without undermining the positive claims already established. Expansion should update the no-list explicitly — not silently erode it.

### Tier 2 — Tier 2 exercises are designed for substantive written responses. Sample answers are provided for Exercises 10 and 15 below; others require student work.

**Exercise 10 — Three Common Failures**

**Failure 1 — Empty or aspirational no-list:**
"We believe in sustainable AI practices" — this is not a no-list item, it is a value statement. A startup that substitutes value statements for no-list items has not made any scope commitments. No investor or engineer reading this strategy can use it to decline any feature request.
*Corrected:* "We do not build tools for consumer end users. We do not accept projects that require real-time processing under 100ms. We do not offer white-glove consulting or implementation services."

**Failure 2 — UVP as feature claim:**
"Our AI processes competitor data 3x faster than the industry average." Speed is a feature. Any competitor can match or beat this claim with a hardware upgrade or model optimization. A UVP is a positioning claim about *who you are for* and *what makes you the only credible choice for that audience*.
*Corrected:* "The only competitive intelligence tool built specifically for the 48-hour product launch cycle — not for quarterly strategy reviews."

**Failure 3 — Archetype inconsistent with positioning:**
Archetype: Outlaw. Positioning: "For Fortune 500 compliance teams who need AI tools that meet regulatory standards." The Outlaw archetype challenges authority; Fortune 500 compliance teams *are* authority. The brand cannot credibly serve both.
*Corrected:* Either change the archetype to Sage (serves compliance teams with analytical rigor) or change the positioning to a market segment where the Outlaw's anti-authority energy is valued (e.g., "For product teams whose internal compliance processes are blocking their AI roadmap").

---

**Exercise 15 — Coherence Failure Repair: Wavefront**

**Question 1 failure:** Not every component expresses the Creator archetype. Values ("Speed, volume, innovation") are partially Magician (innovation, transformation) and partially Ruler (volume, efficiency). A Creator's values should center *craft and output quality* — not speed and volume. "Speed and volume" are the opposite of craft.

**Question 3 failure:** The strategy cannot pass the "could a competitor claim everything" test. "For marketing teams at companies with 500+ employees" + "fastest AI content generator" is generic — every enterprise content AI tool could claim this. There is no specific, owned claim here.

**Minimum revisions:**
1. Values: change "Speed, volume, innovation" to "Craft, originality, output that earns its publish."
2. UVP: change "fastest AI content generator" to "The only AI content tool that enforces brand-voice consistency at the sentence level — not just the template level."
3. No-list: add at minimum four items: "We don't optimize for content volume. We don't serve brands without a documented voice guide. We don't offer template-first content generation. We don't serve B2C brands with undifferentiated voice."

## Instructor Notes

**Prerequisite dependencies:** Chapter 3 (archetype) is required for almost all exercises. Chapter 4 (MVP Boundary) is explicitly required for Exercise 8 and referenced in Exercise 16. Exercise 12 requires Chapter 7 interface layer vocabulary.

**Distinction from Personal Brand Path exercises:** The startup path exercises emphasize the no-list as the primary analytical tool (vs. the personal path's emphasis on seven components + consistency check). Class discussion should foreground the parallel between no-list and MVP Boundary — students who completed Chapter 4 carefully will make this connection quickly.

**Highest-yield exercise for discussion:** Exercise 16 (board member scenario) generates the most productive tension. Students understand abstractly that the no-list is good but feel real pressure when told a competitor has features they don't. The exercise forces them to articulate *why* the no-list is a competitive asset, not just a constraint — this is the hardest concept in the chapter.

**Common student error:** Writing a no-list that is not actually limiting. "We don't build things that harm users" is not a no-list item — no startup would claim to do this. A credible no-list item is one that describes a real market segment or feature type that the startup was genuinely tempted to include and chose to exclude. Instructors should push back on aspirational no-lists that exclude nothing a startup would realistically pursue.
