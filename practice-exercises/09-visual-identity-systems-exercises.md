## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A student has completed a brand strategy (Sage archetype, regulatory intelligence tool, investment analyst audience) and now needs to write a creative brief to guide visual identity development. They have written the following brief excerpt:

> *"We want a modern, clean look with navy blue and white. The typography should be professional. We want it to feel trustworthy and high-quality."*

**Task:** Identify what is wrong with this brief using chapter vocabulary. Then rewrite the relevant sections using the creative brief structure the chapter specifies.

**Model Answer:**

**Problems with the draft brief:**

The draft brief fails on three of the creative brief's eight required sections:

1. **No archetype specification.** The brief does not name the archetype, which means there is no constraint on visual decisions. "Modern and clean" applies to virtually every B2B SaaS tool — it is not an archetypal commitment.

2. **No evidence of strategy-grounding.** "Trustworthy and high-quality" are adjectives without structural roots. The chapter specifies that visual decisions must be grounded in the brand strategy — the adjectives must be derived from archetype, voice, and positioning, not asserted independently.

3. **No negative space.** The brief specifies what is wanted but not what is refused. Without a visual no-list, the designer has no basis for declining visual decisions that feel "also professional."

**Revised brief sections:**

> **Archetype:** Sage
> **Audience:** Quantitative investment analysts at hedge funds and family offices. They evaluate tools analytically, distrust hype, and value source transparency above visual appeal.
> **Visual register:** Information-dense, structured, precision-forward. Every design choice signals that the tool treats data with rigor. No decoration that does not carry information.
> **Color logic:** Primary color derived from Sage archetype palette — deep blue (authority of verified information) with white space used structurally, not decoratively. Accent color only for data highlights, not for brand flourish.
> **Typography principle:** Monospace or serif body text to signal precision and analytical tradition. No display fonts, no rounded corners on type.
> **What this brand refuses visually:** No gradients, no hero illustrations, no icons that personify data, no marketing-page hierarchy. If an element does not communicate information, it is not present.

## Answer Key

### Tier 1

**Exercise 1 — Component Classification**

| Visual Decision | Component |
|---|---|
| A. Documentary-style photography only | Imagery direction |
| B. Navy, white, amber accent | Color palette |
| C. 200ms ease-in on all interactive elements | Motion |
| D. IBM Plex Serif + IBM Plex Mono type pair | Typography |
| E. Geometric mark derived from data flow diagram | Logo/wordmark |
| F. 12-column grid, 8px baseline | Layout system |
| G. Tools, processes, artifacts — never abstract tech | Imagery direction |

**Should be designed last:** Logo/wordmark. The chapter's logic: the logo is the symbolic summary of the visual system, not the starting point. Color, typography, layout, and imagery establish the visual register; the mark should feel like a natural extension of that register. Designing the logo first produces a mark that drives subsequent decisions arbitrarily, rather than summarizing a coherent system.

**Chapter reference:** §1, "Six System Components."

**Exercise 2 — Three Case Studies**

| Case Summary | Company | Year | Failure Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Heritage identity replaced, 20% sales drop | Tropicana | 2009 | Wrong archetype expressed in new packaging — moved from Innocent (simple, pure) toward something that communicated generic commodity, stripping the archetype signal that customers used to identify the product |
| B. CEO cycled 30+ variants with no rationale | Yahoo | 2013 | Strategy-free process — no creative brief, no archetype constraint, so there was no basis for choosing among options; process became arbitrary and the result reflected indecision |
| C. No cars in launch video, flamboyant typography | Jaguar | 2024 | Narrative-archetype mismatch (Chapter 10 primary case): attempted Outlaw/Magician transformation story from a Ruler heritage; visual identity was developed before the product was ready to anchor the transformation claim |

**Exercise 3 — WCAG Standards**

a) 4.5:1 minimum contrast ratio for normal body text under WCAG 2.2 AA.
b) 3:1 minimum contrast ratio for large text (18pt regular or 14pt bold).
c) Light gray (#B0B0B0) on white (#FFFFFF) has an approximate contrast ratio of 2.32:1 — far below both thresholds. The designer should darken the text to at minimum #767676 (passes 4.5:1 on white) for body text, or use the system on a darker background where the gray achieves sufficient contrast.
d) A failing accessibility check signals that the designer has not thought carefully about users — that the portfolio was designed for the designer's aesthetic preference rather than for the range of people who will read it. The chapter specifies this signal is disqualifying in a portfolio designed to be shared widely: it demonstrates indifference to user experience at the surface level.

**Exercise 4 — Creative Brief Eight Sections**

1. **Archetype** — Names the archetype that governs all visual decisions; all other sections derive from this
2. **Audience** — Specifies who will encounter this identity and what they value, scan for, and distrust
3. **Brand strategy summary** — The positioning statement and voice from Chapter 8, compressed to a brief
4. **Color palette rationale** — Primary and accent colors derived from archetype logic, not preference
5. **Typography direction** — Pair rationale grounded in archetype and audience register
6. **Imagery direction** — What visual content the brand uses and refuses to use
7. **Layout system** — Column grid, white space philosophy, information hierarchy
8. **What this brand refuses visually (visual no-list)** — At least three specific visual choices the brand will not make, with archetype-based justifications

**Exercise 5 — Archetype-Palette Logic**

| Archetype | Color Palette | Coherent? | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| a) Sage | Deep blue, white, amber accent | Coherent | Blue signals authority and verified knowledge; white preserves information clarity; amber provides data highlight without hype. All three support analytical, precision-forward register |
| b) Outlaw | Pastels: blush, mint, cream | Incoherent | Pastels communicate softness, approachability, and innocence — the opposite of Outlaw's transgressive, challenging energy. Outlaw palettes typically use high contrast, muted-but-bold, or deliberately unexpected combinations |
| c) Hero | Bold red, black, white | Coherent | High contrast reflects the Hero's clarity and urgency; red signals effort, achievement, and forward momentum; the combination is unambiguous and active |
| d) Caregiver | Warm terracotta, soft sage green, warm white | Coherent | Warm colors signal approachability and attentiveness; sage green adds reliability; warm white avoids clinical coldness. Palette communicates care without sentimentality |
| e) Jester | Neon yellow, electric blue, hot pink | Coherent | High-saturation unexpected combinations communicate irreverence and surprise; the visual register enacts the inversion energy the Jester deploys |
| f) Innocent | Navy, burgundy, gold | Incoherent | Navy/burgundy/gold is Ruler palette — authority, luxury, prestige. The Innocent archetype requires palette choices that communicate simplicity, purity, and safety — lighter tones, softer hues, less formal register |

**Exercise 6 — Typography Pair Logic**

| Archetype | Typographic Register | Common Pairing Mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Sage | Precision-forward: moderate x-height, regularized letterforms, serif or monospace body — typefaces that look like they have been chosen for accuracy and not for flair | Using a trendy humanist sans-serif that feels personable and warm rather than analytical |
| Creator | Expressive and distinctive: typefaces with visible craft decisions, pronounced personality — a display face with character paired with a readable but distinctive body face | Using a generic system font (Arial, Helvetica) that signals "no typographic decision was made" |
| Hero | Active and direct: high contrast between thick and thin strokes, or condensed and bold — suggests forward movement and decisive energy | Using a round, friendly sans-serif that softens urgency and contradicts the Hero's energetic register |
| Everyman | Friendly and familiar: faces that feel like common communication tools — legible, unshowy, trustworthy without being formal | Using a high-craft display face that signals designer aesthetics above accessibility |
| Magician | Transformative and unexpected: faces that carry a slight sense of mystery or unusual geometry — something that feels like it arrived from another register | Using the most obvious "magical" font (an overused calligraphic face) rather than something that creates genuine visual surprise |

**Exercise 7 — Cipe Pineles**

(a) Cipe Pineles was the first woman art director at a major American magazine (Glamour, then Seventeen), who pioneered the integration of fine art illustration with editorial design — establishing that visual identity could carry intellectual and artistic weight, not just functional clarity.

(b) The chapter derives from Pineles the principle that visual identity decisions are intellectual commitments, not decorative choices — that a visual identity system should reflect the same rigor and intentionality as the brand strategy that grounds it, and that the visual system communicates meaning actively, not passively.

---

**Exercise 8 — Wireframe as Archetypal Encoding**

a) Single column, generous white space, most-to-least importance hierarchy → **Sage archetype**. Information is organized for comprehension, not discovery or excitement. White space says "read this carefully." The single column says "there is one thing to understand here." Hierarchy by information density signals that the brand's primary value is analytical clarity.

b) Dense multi-column, multiple panels visible simultaneously, minimal white space → **Ruler archetype** (or Sage in a data-intensive context). Multiple panels signal that the brand's value is comprehensive monitoring; density signals authority and command. The layout communicates "you are in control of a system."

c) Full-bleed hero image, large display text, single CTA below the fold → **Hero or Magician archetype**. The full-bleed image commands immediate attention; the large display text makes one bold claim; the single CTA below the fold places the visitor in a moment of decision. This is the classic transformation offer: "The world is one way; act, and it becomes another."

d) Asymmetric layout, primary content right, strong vertical navigational stripe left → **Creator or Outlaw archetype**. The asymmetry breaks conventional reading conventions deliberately. The navigational stripe suggests structure without conformity. The layout signals a brand that has made unconventional choices and is willing to display them at the layout level.

### Tier 2

**Exercise 10 — Three Problems**

Problem 1 — "I chose blue because it's professional."
Chapter concept violated: Archetype-palette logic. "Professional" is not an archetype commitment — it is a vague adjective that applies to every B2B tool. The chapter specifies that color palette must be derived from archetype, not from adjective. The specific failure: any brand can call itself "professional"; the visual identity loses all differentiation before a user reads a word.

Problem 2 — "I used Helvetica because it's clean and modern."
Chapter concept violated: Typography pair logic. "Clean and modern" is a typography cliché driven by font trends rather than archetype analysis. The chapter specifies that typography must reflect the archetype's register. The specific failure: Helvetica signals "design-trained but not committed" — it is the choice of someone who wants to avoid wrong without attempting right. An archetype-grounded typography decision would be specific about what the type communicates (precision, warmth, urgency, authority) rather than what it avoids (messiness, datedness).

Problem 3 — "I didn't write a creative brief."
Chapter concept violated: Creative brief as constraint system. Without a brief, visual decisions are made by preference and intuition — which means they cannot be defended, revised systematically, or applied consistently across surfaces. The specific failure: when the student deploys to multiple surfaces (portfolio, README, LinkedIn card), there is no documented system to ensure consistency. The visual identity will drift across surfaces.

---

**Exercise 14 — Pepsi 2008 Analysis**

a) The chapter describes the Pepsi rebrand rationale document as invoking "earth's rotational pull, the golden ratio, Leonardo da Vinci, and the Mona Lisa's smile" as justification for the new logo design. These references suggest the designers were working from historical precedent and cultural prestige rather than from Pepsi's brand strategy. No brand strategy component is cited in the rationale.

b) Invoking da Vinci and the Mona Lisa is a symptom of strategy-free design because: when there is no strategy to trace decisions back to, designers seek authority elsewhere. Cultural prestige references (da Vinci, golden ratio) substitute for strategic rationale — they are impressive-sounding justifications that cannot be checked against anything. A strategy-grounded rationale would say: "This curve communicates the Innocent archetype's simplicity and directness because..." A da Vinci rationale cannot be challenged on brand grounds because it is not making a brand argument.

c) Pepsi 2008 and Yahoo 2013: Same failure type — strategy-free process with no archetype constraint. Difference: Pepsi had an elaborate (if pseudo-intellectual) post-hoc rationale; Yahoo had no rationale at all — just a CEO cycling through options. Both produced visual identities disconnected from brand strategy, but through opposite mechanisms: Pepsi over-rationalized without strategic grounding; Yahoo under-rationalized with no constraint at all.

## Instructor Notes

**Prerequisite dependencies:** Chapter 3 (archetype) is required for Exercises 5, 6, 8, 9, 13, 16, and 20. Chapter 8 (brand strategy) is required for Exercises 12, 17, and 20. The creative brief (Exercise 4) cannot be completed without archetype and brand strategy inputs.

**Highest-yield exercise for class discussion:** Exercise 2 (three case studies) generates the richest discussion because students initially attribute the Pepsi failure to design quality rather than strategy. Redirect discussion to: "What would the correct visual identity have looked like, and how would a brief have produced it?"

**Accessibility note:** Exercise 15 (color accessibility check) should be reinforced with the WebAIM Contrast Checker tool in class. Students who run actual checks rather than estimating will often be surprised by how many common combinations fail — particularly light-gray-on-white combinations that look clean but are inaccessible.

**Common student error:** Writing creative briefs that specify color hex codes without rationale. A hex code without an archetype-based rationale is as strategy-free as "I chose blue because it's professional." Push students to write one sentence for each color decision that begins: "This color communicates [archetype register element] because..."

**Tier 4 assessment note:** The archetype-grounding column is the most important element to grade carefully. Students who can trace each visual decision back to a brand strategy component (not just an archetype name) have internalized the chapter's central argument.
