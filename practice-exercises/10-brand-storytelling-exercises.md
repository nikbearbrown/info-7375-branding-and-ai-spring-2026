## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A student with a Sage archetype has built a competitor monitoring tool. They want to write a case study about deploying it for a marketing team. They produce this opening paragraph:

> *"I built a tool that processes competitor data from 15 sources and runs sentiment analysis using GPT-4o-mini. After six weeks of development, I deployed it to our first user. The feedback was positive."*

**Task:** Identify the structural failures in this case study opening using chapter vocabulary. Then rewrite the first paragraph using the customer-as-hero framing.

**Model Answer:**

**Structural failures:**

1. **Brand as hero, not customer.** The opening sentence's subject is "I" — the builder. The customer does not appear in the opening paragraph. Miller's customer-as-hero framing specifies that in a customer story, the customer is always the protagonist; the brand is the guide. This opening violates that framing structurally.

2. **Missing stakes.** "Feedback was positive" is not a stake. The chapter specifies (§3.1 Property 3): "our tool saves time" is not a stake — a stake is specific, visible, and measurable. This opening contains zero evidence of what problem was real for the customer, what they had tried before, or what specifically changed.

3. **Missing customer specificity.** "Our first user" tells the reader nothing. The chapter specifies (§5.3): "the customer's specificity is what makes identification possible." A reader cannot identify with "our first user."

**Revised opening (customer-as-hero):**

*"The marketing manager at a seed-stage SaaS company was spending two hours every Monday morning manually checking six competitor websites, two news feeds, and a Slack channel of screenshots her team sent her over the weekend. By the time she had compiled the weekly briefing, it was 10am and the information was already 72 hours stale. She had tried three different RSS readers and a manual spreadsheet; none of them reduced the time spent below 90 minutes. She needed something that ran without her.*"

This opening: puts the customer in the hero position, specifies the problem with real stakes (2 hours, 72-hour staleness), names what had been tried before, and creates conditions for the guide to enter.

## Answer Key

### Tier 1

**Exercise 1 — Framework Classification**

| Description | Framework |
|---|---|
| A. Customer struggling, receives plan, takes action, transforms | Miller's Customer-as-Hero (StoryBrand) |
| B. Brand arguing the industry is solving the wrong problem; here is the destination | Booker's Quest |
| C. Founder's origin story: ordinary → call → threshold → setbacks → release as gift | Campbell's Hero's Journey |
| D. "You have a problem. Here is a plan." — second-person customer framing | Miller's Customer-as-Hero |
| E. Company's long-running mission toward a destination not yet achieved; products as milestones | Booker's Quest |

**Structural distinctions:**
- Campbell focuses on the hero's *personal transformation*; the journey is about who the hero becomes.
- Booker's Quest focuses on the *importance of the destination*; the goal is the story's spine, not the traveler's growth.
- Miller's Customer-as-Hero repositions the brand from hero to guide — the customer is always the protagonist.

**Exercise 6 — Cadence Over Volume**

a) A consistent monthly piece builds a stable audience signal — people form a model of who you are and what you produce. A burst-then-silence pattern never accumulates: the audience forms a model, then the model goes stale, and the return after silence requires rebuilding the model from scratch.

b) A burst-then-silence pattern tells the story: "This person has enthusiasm but not discipline." This is often worse than no publishing history, because the audience observed the start and can observe the stop.

c) Archetype check: "What story shape is this, and does that shape fit my archetype?" For a Sage: "This is a thought-leadership piece — I am making a falsifiable argument about my domain. The Sage's native story shape is Methodology and Quest; thought leadership is Sage-native. This fits my archetype." **Passes.**

**Exercise 7 — Joseph Campbell**

(a) Campbell argued in *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949) that beneath the surface differences of world mythology, the same structural story — a hero in an ordinary world receives a call, crosses a threshold, faces an ordeal, and returns with a transformative gift — recurs across cultures because it corresponds to the universal human experience of transformation.

(b) The chapter uses Campbell's structural claim to establish that brand story architecture should be built on the same deep structure — not because it is a clever rhetorical trick, but because audiences recognize this structure in themselves. A brand story that follows the structural logic feels true; one that ignores it feels staged.

### Tier 2

**Exercise 10 — Wrong Story Shape for Innocent**

**Pathway activated:** Pathway 3 — the story uses cultural content that belongs to the Outlaw archetype (protest, challenging institutions, fighting back). The Innocent archetype has no claim to this content domain. The anger-and-resistance energy belongs to the Outlaw; when the Innocent attempts it, the borrowing reads as performed outrage.

**Failure mechanism:** The Innocent's deepest commitment is that the world is fundamentally good and the product participates in that goodness. Anger and fighting-back require a world that is fundamentally wrong and an adversary who must be defeated. These are incompatible emotional frames. The audience will feel the inauthenticity even without understanding why.

**Revised opening (Innocent register):**

*"We believe using a tool you trust shouldn't require reading a legal document. We built this so that the experience of competitive intelligence is as clear as the insights it surfaces — nothing hidden, nothing complicated, nothing you wouldn't want to share."*

## Instructor Notes

**Prerequisite dependencies:** Chapter 3 (archetype) is required for Exercises 1, 3, 10, 11, 13, 17, and 18. Chapter 8 (brand strategy) is required for Exercise 12 and the worked example.

**Highest-yield exercise for class discussion:** Exercise 10 (Innocent/Outlaw mismatch) and Exercise 17 (Pepsi analysis) generate the richest discussion. Students often sympathize with Pepsi's intent and resist blaming the campaign for cultural insensitivity. Redirect: the chapter's argument is structural, not ethical. The intent was Innocent (unity, peace); the story shape and cultural content were Outlaw. Three mismatches, one catastrophe — regardless of intent.

**Common student error:** Writing case studies where the guide appears in the story but is still the hero. A student writes "the customer had a problem; we solved it for them" — the customer is present but passive. The customer must be the protagonist who acts, struggles, and transforms. The guide enables the hero to act; the guide does not act for the hero.

**Tier 4 grading note:** The thought-leadership thesis is the most difficult element for students. Generic theses ("AI is changing everything") are nearly universal. Push students to reach for falsifiability: if a hostile reader with access to data could disprove the thesis, it is specific enough. If the thesis is merely an observation that any informed reader would agree with, it is not a thesis.
