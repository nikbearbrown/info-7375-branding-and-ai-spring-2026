## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A student has built the following portfolio:

> - 11 projects, each with a two-sentence description and a thumbnail
> - Projects include: e-commerce site (React, 2020), a mobile weather app (React Native, 2021), a "random recipe generator" (Python, 2022), a course machine-learning project, a competitor monitoring AI tool (the tool built in this course), a data visualization dashboard, three team projects from prior jobs, and two side projects
> - Portfolio headline: "Software Engineer | AI | Full-Stack | Python | JavaScript | React"
> - No positioning sentence
> - No origin story
> - One case study (the competitor monitoring tool) at 150 words
> - Deployed at: `github.io/studentname`

**Task:** Identify the three most significant failures using chapter vocabulary. Prioritize the failures from most to least structurally damaging.

**Model Answer:**

**Failure 1 — Portfolio is a resume with images, not a product (most damaging).**
The chapter specifies (§1): "portfolio-as-product" requires that a recruiter encountering the portfolio can identify the archetype, positioning, and strongest project unprompted in two minutes (the Feynman test). This portfolio fails the Feynman test: 11 equal-weight projects with no curation signal produce no archetype signal and no point of view. The chapter's curation rule (§3): three to six projects — three is better than six if the three are excellent. Eleven projects at 150-word descriptions each is "I have done things," not "I am this kind of engineer."

**Failure 2 — No positioning sentence; headline is a skill list.**
The chapter specifies (§7, LinkedIn optimization) that the headline should be a positioning sentence, not a job title or skill list. "Software Engineer | AI | Full-Stack | Python | JavaScript | React" tells a recruiter nothing about what the engineer is *for* — who they serve, what problem they solve, what makes them non-interchangeable. Without a positioning sentence, Channel 2 (indirect reference) is impossible — you cannot reference someone you cannot describe.

**Failure 3 — Case study at 150 words fails all four functions.**
The chapter specifies (§4) the AI tool case study must perform four functions: frame the problem, show the technical work, connect to brand, and be honest about limits. 150 words cannot perform all four. The chapter specifies 800–1,500 words for this case study. A case study that cannot frame the problem, show the architecture, and document the transformation in the same artifact is incomplete as a portfolio centerpiece.

## Answer Key

### Tier 1

**Exercise 1 — Five Artifacts**

| Description | Artifact |
|---|---|
| A. ATS-optimized text document, 30 seconds | Resume |
| B. Designed work presentation at personal URL, 2–5 minutes | Portfolio website |
| C. Code work, public, technical credibility | GitHub profile |
| D. Recruiter search discovery, first surface visited | LinkedIn profile |
| E. Coherent brand artifact across multiple surfaces | Portfolio-as-product |

**Most commonly designed well:** Resume — most students invest in this because it is the most familiar artifact.
**Most commonly underused:** LinkedIn profile. The chapter notes most graduates treat LinkedIn as a checkbox — the minimum changes (headline, about section, featured section) take 2–3 hours but have compounding returns because recruiters encounter it first and most often.

---

**Exercise 2 — Three Channels**

| Channel | Name | Primary Agent | Time Frame | Portfolio Property |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Direct hiring | Recruiter with open role | Immediate | Recruiter-legible: clear positioning, standard sections, working links |
| 2 | Indirect reference | Developer, manager, professor — without an open role | Delayed (weeks to months) | Worth referencing: distinctive, coherent, well-executed — memorable |
| 3 | Template effects | Other developers building portfolios | Autonomous, scales over time | Clonable: clean design, open source code, simple enough to template |

---

**Exercise 3 — Exclusion Rationale**

a) To-do list app (React tutorial, 2021) — **Exclude.** Including it signals that the engineer's bar for what counts as a project includes tutorials, which dilutes every other project on the list.

b) Team dashboard (non-AI, SQL + Tableau) — **Exclude.** This project demonstrates SQL and BI skills, not AI and pipeline skills. Including it undermines the "developer-first marketing intelligence" positioning by implying the engineer also does standard BI work.

c) Sentiment analysis on movie reviews (Kaggle) — **Exclude** (borderline). Sentiment analysis is archetype-relevant, but movie reviews have no relationship to marketing intelligence. Including it risks diluting the focused positioning without adding meaningful differentiation.

d) RAG-based document Q&A (internal customer support) — **Include.** RAG + production deployment + business context (customer support is adjacent to intelligence tools) — this strengthens the positioning and shows production-grade AI work.

e) Competitor monitoring AI tool (this course) — **Include.** This is the flagship project; it directly expresses the archetype and positioning.

---

**Exercise 4 — Four Functions**

| Function | Present | Absent: Failure |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Frame the problem | A specific user, a specific pain, a specific frequency, real stakes — the reader can find ten people who fit the description before finishing the sentence | The case study is unreadable to non-technical evaluators; there is no hook, no reason to keep reading |
| 2. Show the technical work | Architecture decision, key tool choices with one-sentence justifications, specific metrics (throughput, latency, cost, accuracy) | The case study cannot be distinguished from a marketing claim; there is no evidence the engineer understands what they built |
| 3. Connect to brand | A note on archetype, voice, visual choices; what the $100,000 no was; what the alignment audit revealed | The case study reads as a purely technical artifact; it does not demonstrate that the engineer thinks about engineering as design |
| 4. Be honest about limits | What the tool does not do; what the MVP boundary excluded and why; what Build-Measure-Learn has revealed | The case study overpromises; trust in the positive claims is reduced because the engineer appears unable to assess their own work's limits |

---

**Exercise 5 — v0 vs. Framer**

a) v0 gives more implementation control (exports real React code you can extend) and sends a stronger technical signal to React-oriented engineering teams; Framer gives more precise visual control and is faster for design-first users, but hosts on Framer's infrastructure and signals design-tool proficiency rather than engineering depth.

b) A student who deploys v0 output without customization has a portfolio that is "competent and on-trend" but looks like other v0 portfolios. The distinction that enables Channel 2 and Channel 3 compounding comes from the brand strategy, visual identity, and content the student applies on top of the tool. The tool is the scaffold; the semester's work is the differentiation.

c) Hand-coded React is justified when: (1) the student is applying to React-focused engineering teams where the portfolio itself is a code signal, and (2) the student has sufficient React proficiency to produce a clean, well-structured codebase that would impress a code reviewer.

---

**Exercise 6 — Coherence Audit**

The four items from the chapter (§6):

1. **Positioning sentence check.** Binary question: "Does the one-sentence positioning on the portfolio's above-the-fold match the LinkedIn headline?"

2. **Archetype legibility check.** Binary question: "Can a recruiter who reads both the portfolio and the LinkedIn profile identify the same archetype from both — without being told what the archetype is?"

3. **Project consistency check.** Binary question: "Are the same three to five projects featured in the portfolio's project section, the LinkedIn featured section, and the resume's project entries?"

4. **Voice consistency check.** Binary question: "Does reading the portfolio About section and the LinkedIn About section aloud back-to-back sound like the same person?"

---

**Exercise 7 — Brittany Chiang**

a) Chiang's portfolio performed in Channel 3 because it was clean, minimal, dark-themed, and open-source. The design was simple enough to clone (no complex custom elements, no unique framework dependencies) and distinctive enough to be worth cloning (a specific aesthetic that was better than most first-portfolio attempts). The GitHub repo was public and well-documented.

b) The chapter describes Chiang's career moving through Upstatement, Apple, Spotify, and senior roles at Klaviyo. The chapter uses this to suggest the portfolio compounded alongside (not instead of) excellent engineering work and a strong network.

c) The chapter's "What would change my mind" note says: Chiang's portfolio is consistent with the compounding hypothesis but does not prove it — she had excellent technical skills, good timing, and a strong network that may have been more causal. A study comparing hiring outcomes for engineers with high-craft vs. adequate portfolios, controlling for technical skill, would either strengthen or weaken the chapter substantially.

**Exercise 8 — What Does Not Belong**

1. **Screenshot wall of every project** — taxes visitor attention; no point of view; signals "I did things" not "I am this kind of engineer"
2. **Skills section listing 50 technologies as icons** — dilutes every claimed skill; icons without context cannot be evaluated; signals lack of focus
3. **Personal hobbies unrelated to the archetype** — adds noise without archetype signal; a Sage AI engineer's hiking hobby does not reinforce the Sage brand
4. **Long lists of job titles and dates** — this is the resume's job; repeating it on the portfolio without narrative adds length without depth
5. **"Currently learning X" list** — implies the skill is not yet learned; signals a work in progress rather than a deployed practitioner

**Exercise 10 — Coherence Failure Analysis**

**Failure 2 — Resume headline is generic.**

## Instructor Notes

**Prerequisite dependencies:** Chapter 3 (archetype) is required for Exercises 3, 9, 14, and 20. Chapter 8-9 (brand strategy + visual identity) are required for Exercises 10 and 20. Chapter 10 (storytelling) is required for Exercise 12. The whole course chain is required for Tier 4.

**Highest-yield exercise for class discussion:** Exercise 17 (counter-argument: when adequacy is correct) forces students to think carefully about when the chapter's advice applies vs. when it is overkill. This exercise reveals which students have internalized the chapter as a framework vs. adopted it as a rule.

**Feynman test in class:** Exercise 16 (designing the Feynman test) and the Tier 4 Feynman test reporting are most valuable when done live in class — students test each other's portfolios and report back. The most common finding is that archetype is legible but positioning is not, or that positioning is present but the strongest project is not clearly identified.

**Common student error:** Including too many projects and calling it comprehensive. The chapter is explicit: three is better than six if the three are excellent. Students resist cutting work they are proud of — the best response is to ask: "Which projects would a recruiter reference to a colleague? Feature those three."
