## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A student has built a strong AI tool and a coherent portfolio, but their final presentation has the following structure:

> - Slide 1: "My Presentation — [Name]"
> - Slide 2: "About Me" — 3 paragraphs of biographical background, degrees, prior jobs
> - Slide 3: "The Tool I Built" — a list of features
> - Slide 4: Technical architecture diagram (no labels, very dense)
> - Slide 5: A screenshot
> - Slide 6: "My Brand" — archetype named, voice described
> - Slide 7: "Challenges I Faced" — three technical difficulties encountered during development
> - Slide 8: "What's Next" — a 12-feature roadmap
> - Slide 9: "Skills" — 30 technologies listed in a grid
> - Slide 10: "Thank You! Questions?"

**Task:** Identify the three most significant structural failures using chapter vocabulary and the Kawasaki/Airbnb framework.

**Model Answer:**

**Failure 1 — Missing problem slide; biographical opening wastes the first sixty seconds.**
The chapter specifies: "Open in scene, not in introduction" (§4, Opening and Closing). The first sixty seconds are when the audience decides whether to pay attention. Slide 1-2 (title + biographical background) wastes those sixty seconds entirely. There is no problem slide — the presentation never establishes who hurts, how much, or how often. Without a problem slide, the tool slide has no frame, the architecture has no justification, and the ask (if it existed) would land with no urgency.

The Airbnb principle: "Problem implies solution." Without a problem slide, the entire rest of the argument is unanchored.

**Failure 2 — Ten different arguments instead of one.**
The chapter specifies (§1, Coherence Principle): most pitches fail because each slide is a separate plea. This deck makes at least five different arguments: "here is who I am" (biographical), "here is what the tool does" (feature list), "here is how it works technically" (architecture), "here was my journey" (challenges), "here is my roadmap" (features). No slide leans on the previous one. The audience leaves with fragments, not an argument.

**Failure 3 — Skills grid and "Thank You! Questions?" closing.**
The chapter specifies the ask must be specific and direct (§4, Slide 10). "Thank you! Questions?" produces vague responses — the audience has no instruction for what to do with what they just saw. A specific ask ("I am looking for a machine-learning engineering role at a startup building user-facing AI products. If that describes you or someone you know, I'd like fifteen minutes") converts the presentation into an actionable invitation.

The 30-technology skills grid violates the coherence principle: it diffuses the archetype signal exactly when the "You as the team" slide (§4, Slide 9) should be doing the most persuasion work.

## Tier 4 — Evaluate and Create (Capstone)

*(Tests: LO1-LO6 — full chapter application)*

**Task:** Produce and deliver the complete launch package.

Your launch package must include:
1. A ten-slide, twenty-minute pitch deck using the chapter's slide structure
2. Speaker notes for each slide (what you say in the 1.5–2 minutes per slide)
3. One full timed practice run (record yourself; watch it back; identify the one slide you are reading from rather than presenting)
4. An ATS-optimized resume (single column, outcome-oriented bullets, no made-up numbers)
5. A designer resume (brand visual system applied, same content as ATS version)
6. A launch post for LinkedIn (or Substack) — opens in scene, includes portfolio URL and tool URL, ends with specific ask
7. A post-course plan with three portfolio additions, three published pieces, and five relationship engagements, all with specific deadlines

**Presentation delivery requirements:**
- Opens in scene (not in introduction)
- Runs specific numbers on at least Slides 2, 5, and 7
- Closes with a direct, specific ask
- Timing: at or under 20 minutes in full run-through

**Evaluation criteria (no model answer — assessed by rubric):**

| Dimension | Developing | Proficient | Exemplary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Argument coherence | Slides argue multiple separate things | All slides reinforce one argument | Each slide explicitly leans on the previous; no slide could be removed without weakening the argument |
| Specific numbers | Vague claims ("strong performance") | Numbers on at least 2 slides | Specific numbers on Slides 2, 5, 7; each number is real or has a verify placeholder |
| Scene opening | Biographical introduction | Scene present but generic | Specific person, specific moment, specific pain — the problem is visible before the tool is named |
| Specific ask | "Open to opportunities" | Role type specified | Role type, company type, and preferred next step specified; deliverable in one sentence |
| Resume outcomes | Responsibility bullets | Outcome bullets on most entries | All bullets show scale + result; no made-up numbers (verify placeholders used where needed) |
| Post-course plan | Aspirational list without deadlines | Commitments with dates | Commitments with dates + one-sentence reasoning for each item |

## Answer Key

### Tier 1

**Exercise 1 — Airbnb Deck Three Properties**

| Property | Airbnb Deck | Vague Version |
|---|---|---|
| Argument coherence | Every slide carries the same argument: "the world wants this, here is the proof, we are the team." Problem implies solution; solution implies market; market implies team. | Ten slides making ten separate arguments: problem, architecture, brand story, career background, market size, etc. Audience leaves remembering nothing because there was no single thread. |
| Specific numbers | Real beta data from San Francisco and Denver runs — small numbers but specific. "87 bookings in the first six weeks" is a claim a reviewer can picture and audit. | "Strong early user interest" — a claim that signals the founders have not yet done the work. No specific numbers means no auditable evidence. |
| Team weight | Three named founders with specific, relevant prior work. The team slide is short and specific — it signals that these people can execute on the specific claims made earlier. | "Experienced team of builders" — generic, signals nothing. Without named, specific, relevant prior work, the team slide is a placeholder that undermines rather than supports the pitch. |

**Exercise 5 — Scene Openings**

a) Generic: "Hi, I'm [name] and today I'll be presenting my AI tool."
Scene: "It's 7:45 on a Tuesday. A marketing manager opens her laptop. Before she can do any strategy work, she has to spend 40 minutes checking what her competitors shipped overnight. My tool does that in 90 seconds. Here is how."

c) Generic: "Today I'll walk you through my project and brand strategy."

**Exercise 7 — Margaret Bourke-White**

(a) Bourke-White deliberately chose assignments that crossed institutional boundaries — first foreign photographer in the Soviet Union, first woman war correspondent with the U.S. Army Air Forces, first female photographer at *Life* magazine — building a professional presence through intentional boundary-crossing rather than waiting for the assignments to be offered.

(b) The chapter derives from this the principle that professional presence is an assembled artifact built through intentional decisions about what to take on and what to refuse — not a byproduct of doing good work and waiting to be recognized. The resume, the deck, and the social-media stack are built by the same deliberate discipline Bourke-White applied to her assignment choices.

**Exercise 8 — Three Post-Course Habits**

1. **Refresh the portfolio quarterly.** Specific action this week: schedule a calendar reminder in 90 days with a specific task: "Add one case study to portfolio, update bio, review oldest project for replacement."

2. **Publish on a sustainable cadence.** Specific action this week: commit to a specific first piece with a publish date within 30 days and write the first draft this week — not after the course ends, while the material is live.

3. **Build relationships, not network.** Specific action this week: identify five people whose work was encountered during this course; engage with one piece of their work today in a way that shows close reading (not "great post" — one specific thought, one question, one additional data point).

### Tier 2

**Exercise 10 — Coherence Failures**

**Failure 1 — Argument coherence: Slide 3 problem statement is generic.**
Property violated: Argument coherence. "AI is making knowledge work more efficient" is an observation that every AI tool in the world could put on its problem slide. It does not imply this tool. The Airbnb principle: problem implies solution. A generic problem does not imply a specific solution.
Fix: Replace with a specific scene (one person, one morning, one pain + one number).

**Failure 2 — Specific numbers: absent across the deck.**
Property violated: Specific numbers. No metrics are mentioned anywhere in the deck summary. The chapter specifies specific numbers are required on at least Slides 2, 5, and 7.
Fix: Add at minimum: a time/cost metric on the problem slide, a throughput or latency metric on Slide 5, and a user feedback metric on Slide 7.

**Failure 3 — Team weight: skills grid instead of specific capacity.**
Property violated: Team weight. "I know Python, JavaScript, React, TensorFlow, and SQL" is a skills list, not a team argument. At the stage of a course project, the "team slide" argument is about what this person specifically has built and shipped — not what technologies they can list. The portfolio URL (mentioned in the chapter for Slide 9) is the strongest evidence available.
Fix: Remove the skills grid. Replace with: archetype, one sentence about what the student builds, and the portfolio URL. The portfolio is the evidence.

**Failure 4 — Specific ask: "I am open to any opportunities."**
Property violated: Argument coherence (the ask must close the argument, not open a new one). A vague ask makes the presentation end in ambiguity — the audience has no instruction.
Fix: "I am looking for a machine-learning engineering role at a startup that is building user-facing AI products. If that describes you or someone you know, I would like fifteen minutes."

## Instructor Notes

**Prerequisite dependencies:** All prior chapters are implicitly required for the Tier 4 capstone. Chapter 8 (brand strategy) is required for Slides 6 and 9. Chapter 9 (visual identity) is required for the designer resume. Chapter 10 (storytelling) is required for the scene opening and case study references. Chapter 11 (portfolio) is required for the portfolio URL on Slide 9.

**Highest-yield exercise for class discussion:** Exercise 17 (coherence applied across artifacts) and the presentation practice in Exercise 13. The most consistent class observation: students understand the coherence principle abstractly but write vague asks in their actual presentations. Run the ask slide in class — ask each student to say their Slide 10 ask aloud. Vague asks will be immediately apparent; specific asks will be immediately distinguishable.

**30-point font rule in class:** Kawasaki's 30-point font rule should be enforced in the actual final presentation. A student who uses font smaller than 30 is reading from the slide. Instructors can use this as a diagnostic: if a student needs 12-point font to fit their content, they have not yet edited the content to presentation density.

**Post-course plan accountability:** The post-course plan (Exercise 15, Tier 4) is most effective if students share it publicly or with a partner. The chapter's observation that cadence beats quality means that sharing the commitment creates a social accountability mechanism that improves follow-through. Consider requiring students to post their post-course plan to LinkedIn as their launch post.
