## Answer Key

> Read this section only after completing all questions.

---

**Q1 — B**
*Correct because:* The chapter explicitly states that "brand archetypes are claims about how brands organize identity and how customers form recognition" — these are the Mark/Pearson framework's claims, distinct from Jungian psychology (claims about the collective unconscious) or personality typology (claims about individual people). The chapter notes that Mark and Pearson's connection to Jung is useful for explaining why patterns feel familiar, but "the work itself is brand strategy, not psychology."
*Why the distractors are wrong:*
- A) This describes Jungian psychological archetypes — the chapter distinguishes this as meaning 1 of "archetype" and explicitly separates it from the brand-strategy framework.
- C) This describes personality typology archetypes (Myers-Briggs style) — the chapter identifies this as "meaning 2" used in team building and self-knowledge, not brand strategy.
*Chapter reference:* Part I — What an Archetype Actually Is, Section 1.1

---

**Q2 — C**
*Correct because:* The chapter's five-input procedure states: "Archetypes are as legible in exclusions as in inclusions. Nike will not run a campaign built around rest and acceptance — that is antithetical to the Hero's commitments." Reading what a brand refuses to do reveals the archetype's active commitments from the negative space, the same way the absence of tutorials in Engineer A's GitHub revealed her Rebel archetype (from Chapter 1's parallel argument).
*Why the distractors are wrong:*
- A) Budget priorities and resource constraints are not mentioned as outputs of the exclusions analysis; the chapter treats exclusions as identity signals, not financial signals.
- B) The shadow prediction comes from step 4 of the shadow-analysis procedure (naming the archetype, then inferring the shadow), not directly from reading exclusions.
*Chapter reference:* Part IV — Applying the Framework, Section 4.1, Input 3

---

**Q3 — False**
*Correct because:* The chapter explicitly states: "They were not rejecting the new design because it was ugly; many design critics praised it." The rejection was because customers' recognition handles — the orange-with-straw image trained over two decades — had been removed, and customers could not locate the brand they had built a model of. The mechanism was recognition failure, not aesthetic judgment.
*Chapter reference:* Part III, Section 3.2 — Tropicana, 2009

---

**Q4 — B**
*Correct because:* The chapter defines the Caregiver's shadow as "martyrdom, enabling" and describes it as: "a Caregiver brand that drifts develops a tone of 'we sacrifice for you, and you should notice that.' It becomes manipulative and exhausting." This is structurally derived from the Caregiver's strength (generosity and service) taken too far into self-congratulatory sacrifice.
*Why the distractors are wrong:*
- A) Rigidity is the Ruler archetype's shadow — defending structure past its useful life — not the Caregiver's.
- C) Denial is the Innocent archetype's shadow — refusing to acknowledge complexity or bad news — not the Caregiver's.
*Chapter reference:* Part I, Section 1.2 — The twelve archetypes; Part II, Section 2.3 — The shadow as failure-mode prediction

---

**Q5 — True**
*Correct because:* The chapter states this directly: "an archetype is a forcing function. It is not a brand asset in the sense of a logo or a tagline. It is the constraint that makes all the other brand assets cohere." The chapter also states the TL;DR of the chapter: "A brand archetype is not a personality quiz. It is a consistency-enforcement device — the constraint that makes every downstream decision decidable."
*Chapter reference:* Part II, Section 2.1 — The brand decision problem

---

**Q6 — B**
*Correct because:* The chapter's Tropicana case is the direct template for this scenario. Bright colors, rounded forms, literal imagery, simple messaging, and optimistic campaigns are hallmarks of the Innocent archetype ("good and natural"). Moving to sophisticated typography, moody photography, and a tagline about personal identity signals ("What you choose says everything") is the visual and verbal language of Ruler or Lover — a category-error drift, not a shadow drift. The recognition handles built over fifteen years are being replaced with signals from a different archetype the brand has no equity in.
*Why the distractors are wrong:*
- A) The Innocent's shadow is denial — refusing to acknowledge bad news — which is not what a new agency campaign with sophisticated aesthetics expresses; that is an archetype-direction change, not shadow activation.
- C) Moving toward Sage would involve evidence-based, authoritative content about nutrition — not moody photography and identity-statement taglines; the description matches Ruler/Lover more closely.
*Chapter reference:* Part III, Section 3.2 — Tropicana drift analysis; Part IV, Section 4.1

---

**Q7 — Short-Answer**
*Model answer:* The chapter identifies Coca-Cola as an Innocent brand (organized around sameness, tradition, and wholesome identity) and Pepsi as operating in the Outlaw register (challenger to the establishment, "the choice of the new generation"). The Outlaw archetype draws energy from the existence of something worth disrupting — specifically, the establishment's refusal to change. When Coca-Cola changed its formula to compete on Pepsi's terms, it simultaneously abandoned the Innocent's core commitment (sameness is the product's deepest value) and eliminated the contrast that gave Pepsi its Outlaw identity. The Innocent brand cannot win an Outlaw competition because becoming a challenger means abandoning what made it the Innocent. Pepsi no longer needed to claim it was newer; Coca-Cola's behavior confirmed it.
*Common error:* Describing the error as a product or taste preference mistake rather than an archetype incompatibility — the chapter explicitly frames it as a category error, not a market research failure.
*Chapter reference:* Part III, Section 3.4 — New Coke, 1985

---

**Q8 — B**
*Correct because:* The chapter states the recognition asset "requires that the hundredth touchpoint is consistent with the first. An archetype is the cheapest instrument available for enforcing that consistency, because it operates at the level of identity rather than execution. You do not have to tell every designer, writer, and engineer to produce exactly this output. You tell them who the brand is. A designer who knows the brand is a Sage will make different choices — consistently — than a designer who knows the brand is a Jester, even without a style guide."
*Why the distractors are wrong:*
- A) The chapter does not describe archetypes as sign-off documents; the mechanism is identity internalization, not procedural approval.
- C) The chapter does not claim archetypes reduce the number of touchpoints needed; it claims they make the touchpoints that do occur consistent.
*Chapter reference:* Part II, Section 2.2 — The recognition asset

---

**Q9 — Short-Answer**
*Model answer:* A Hero brand positions its marketing around the customer's potential for mastery and achievement, not around the brand's own qualities — the brand is the enablement, not the protagonist. Nike markets "Just Do It," not "Nike is excellent." For a product failure announcement, a Hero brand would respond with a challenge-and-resolution framing: "Here is the obstacle, here is how we overcame it, here is what this means for your performance." An Innocent brand would respond with reassurance and simplicity: "Here is what went wrong, we fixed it, everything is good and safe again." The Hero's response positions the customer as capable of handling the bad news and moving forward; the Innocent's response restores the sense of uncomplicated goodness that the Innocent archetype promises.
*What a strong answer includes:*
- The "customer is the hero, brand is the equipment" translation into marketing positioning
- A concrete comparison between Hero and Innocent crisis-response tone
- Use of the chapter's five-input procedure logic (crisis response reveals archetype)
*Chapter reference:* Part I, Section 1.3 — What the names hide; Part IV, Section 4.1, Input 5

---

**Q10 — Brief-Response**
*Model answer:* The chapter provides specific evidence that preference is not the mechanism: in the Tropicana case, "many design critics praised" the new design and customers did not reject it on aesthetic grounds — they stopped recognizing it as Tropicana. In the New Coke case, the reformulated product "performed well" in blind taste tests; customers preferred it when they did not know what they were drinking. The rejection was specifically about brand identity, not taste preference. The chapter's mechanism — the recognition asset accumulated over decades — predicts that customers would reject the change regardless of their objective preference for the new product, and this prediction is confirmed by both cases. The classmate's "preference" explanation cannot account for why blind-taste-test winners failed in the market.
*What a strong answer includes:*
- The New Coke blind-taste-test evidence as the clearest counter to the preference explanation
- The Tropicana "critics praised it" evidence
- The recognition-asset mechanism as the alternative explanation
*Common error:* Conceding that preference might explain some cases without using the chapter's evidence to distinguish the mechanism.
*Chapter reference:* Part III, Sections 3.2 and 3.4; Part II, Section 2.2

---

**Q11 — Transfer Brief-Response**
*Model answer:* The proposed shift most clearly fails the authenticity test and the differentiation test from the chapter's three-test framework. The authenticity test asks whether the new archetype matches how the brand actually behaves under pressure — a Sage brand built on authoritative, evidence-first content for litigators and compliance teams has its identity in rigor, not accessibility; a forced shift to humor and approachable onboarding is unlikely to be sustainable because it runs counter to the brand's accumulated identity signals. The differentiation test asks whether the new archetype distinguishes the brand from competitors: the legal-tech market for students and paralegals may already have accessible, approachable entrants, meaning a Sage brand moving there is entering a more crowded position rather than finding an unoccupied one. The stress test would ask whether the new archetype survives growth — and a brand that has licensed its credibility from "trusted by litigators" may find that the student-friendly voice undermines its authority positioning with existing customers.
*What a strong answer includes:*
- Naming at least two of the three tests explicitly and applying them to the scenario
- The authenticity test argument: the Sage's identity is in rigor, not approachability
- The differentiation test argument: the accessible/approachable position may be more crowded
- Recognition that the shift risks the existing customer's recognition asset (the litigator audience)
*Chapter reference:* Part V, Section 5.1 — What you are actually deciding; the three tests
