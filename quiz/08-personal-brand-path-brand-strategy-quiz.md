## Section 1 — Recall & Recognition

**Q1.** According to the chapter, which of the seven brand strategy components is described as the "most structurally important output of a brand strategy document"?

- A) Archetype
- B) UVP
- C) Negative-space list (the things the company will not do)

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** The chapter states explicitly: "A brand is more defined by what it declines than by what it does." The negative-space list is the mechanism that makes identity legible through consistency of constraint, not volume of production. Archetype (A) is the strategic anchor, not the structural load-bearer; UVP (B) describes differentiation, not the enforcement mechanism.

---

**Q2.** Stripe's mission — "Increase the GDP of the internet" — is held up as a model. Which property does the chapter say a good mission must have that "Make payments easier" lacks?

- A) It must reference a specific technology (APIs, payments infrastructure)
- B) It must be testable and falsifiable — referring to something that could in principle be measured
- C) It must be short enough to fit on a single slide

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter says "Make payments easier" fails because it is not testable — easier than what, for whom, by how much? Falsifiability makes a mission a real constraint. Technology reference (A) is actually a failure mode — the chapter notes good missions are technology-agnostic. Length (C) is not the issue raised.

---

**Q3.** True or False: According to the chapter, a company's archetype and its founders' personal archetypes are always the same.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter is explicit: "The company archetype and the founder's archetype often overlap, but they are not identical." It gives the example of a Rebel founder who can lead a Caregiver company if the product's audience requires it, and warns that the reverse — a Caregiver founder leading a Rebel brand — is the more common and problematic failure.

---

**Q4.** The chapter describes four questions for matching archetype to audience. Which question is listed as pointing toward the Sage archetype for developer audiences?

- A) What does this audience fear?
- B) What does this audience distrust?
- C) What does this audience reward?

**Answer:** C
**Rationale:** The chapter's Figure 8.5 table shows that developers "reward" demonstrated depth — code, docs, post-mortems — which points toward the Sage archetype. Fear (A) maps to the role that "answers fear"; distrust (B) identifies what archetype to avoid expressing.

---

**Q5.** True or False: According to the chapter, a negative-space item that says "We will not build features that compromise simplicity" passes the specificity criterion.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter explicitly distinguishes decorative from specific constraints: "'We won't build features that compromise simplicity' is decorative. 'We will not add a settings panel with more than five options in v1' is specific." Specific means a new engineer could make a decision from it. The vague version cannot be violated, which means it provides no real constraint.

## Section 2 — Application & Analysis

**Q6.** Stripe declined enterprise sales processes for its first several years, losing deals that required account management and bespoke configurations. In the chapter's framework, which negative-space criterion does this "no" best exemplify?

- A) Testable over time — a reader in two years can verify whether the commitment held
- B) Costly — the business pressure to say yes was real, and the refusal was a genuine commitment
- C) Specific — a new engineer reading it would know what to do with a borderline request

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter's criterion for "costly" is that "saying yes is genuinely tempting — there is real business pressure." Stripe was declining deals that required account management; the pressure was real. The item also passes testable (A) and specific (C) — but the chapter's framing of this particular example emphasizes the real business cost of declining enterprise contracts when they were being offered.

---

**Q7.** The chapter describes a "shadow" for every archetype as a known failure mode. Apply this concept: Stripe is identified as a Sage company. What specific Sage shadow risk does the chapter name, and what product behavior illustrates it?

**Model Answer:** The chapter names the Sage shadow as dogmatism — "so committed to depth and rigor that the company becomes rigid, fails to iterate fast enough, or dismisses feedback that contradicts its model of the world." The chapter's concrete illustration is Stripe's API versioning policy: maintaining every API version indefinitely rather than deprecating old ones. This rigor serves developers who built on old versions, but it also makes the codebase substantially more complex. The shadow is the cost of the strength.

---

**Q8.** A student drafts a negative-space list for their AI tool company and shows it to a classmate without the rest of the strategy document. The classmate cannot infer the company's archetype from the list. According to the chapter, which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) The archetype is too rare — classmates won't know the 12-archetype taxonomy well enough
- B) The list is too vague or too random to encode identity — items fail the archetype-consistency criterion
- C) The student should have shown the values section alongside the list for context

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states explicitly: "The useful test: show your draft negative-space list to a classmate without the rest of the strategy document. Ask them to infer your archetype from the list alone. If they guess correctly, the list is doing its job. If they cannot tell, the list is too vague or too random to encode identity." This is an archetype-consistency failure, not a taxonomy knowledge problem (A) or a context problem (C).

---

**Q9.** The chapter describes a "values test": for a value to be real rather than decorative, it must meet a specific condition. What is that condition, and how does the chapter apply it to Stripe's documentation value?

**Model Answer:** The chapter's test is that a real value is one the company "maintains when maintaining them is costly." A value that has never cost anything is a preference, not a value. Applied to Stripe: "Documentation as product" is real because Stripe declines to ship a feature until the documentation is done — even when that delays the release. Engineers who could be shipping features instead maintain and improve docs. The cost is the proof of the commitment.

## Section 3 — Synthesis & Evaluation

**Q10.** A product manager argues: "The one-page constraint is just a formatting preference. A thorough brand strategy document should be as long as it needs to be to cover all the nuances." The chapter explicitly disagrees. Construct a counter-argument using the chapter's reasoning, and identify what a document longer than one page reveals about the quality of the strategy itself.

**Model Answer:** The chapter's counter-argument is that the one-page constraint is "a constraint, not a convenience" — and that if the strategy does not fit on one page, "you have not yet made enough decisions. You are still listing options rather than committing to positions." A document longer than one page is not more thorough; it is less specified. Every section that runs long contains hedges and explanatory prose where a specific commitment should be. The compression is the work — a long mission section probably has two hedges and one real claim; the job is to find the claim and cut the hedges. Length is evidence of insufficient decision-making, not thoroughness.

**Q11.** A Creator-archetype founder is building a solo AI writing assistant for academic researchers. She has drafted her negative-space list with five items. Item 3 reads: "We will not build collaboration features or real-time co-editing, even if research team users request it." Apply the four negative-space criteria from the chapter (specific, archetype-consistent, costly, testable) to evaluate whether this item is doing its job. Then identify what a Creator-archetype brand should be able to predict from this refusal.

**Model Answer:** The item passes all four criteria. Specific: a new engineer knows exactly what to decline when a research team asks for Google Docs-like co-editing. Archetype-consistent: the Creator archetype's core is individual craft and creative autonomy; a tool for solo deep work that resists collaboration features is a predictable Creator expression — the Creator does not dilute focus with social infrastructure. Costly: collaboration features are a common request from expanding research teams; a competitor might add them to expand TAM. The business pressure to say yes is real. Testable: two years from now, a reader can check whether real-time co-editing was ever shipped.

From this refusal, a Creator-brand should be able to predict: the tool will prioritize depth of individual capability over breadth of team features; it will not chase the enterprise collaboration market that teams like Notion or Overleaf serve; its UVP is the quality of the solo thinking experience, not the efficiency of group workflows. The refusal encodes the audience (solo researchers, not research teams), the competitive position (not vs. Overleaf), and the archetype commitment (individual craft over social coordination).
