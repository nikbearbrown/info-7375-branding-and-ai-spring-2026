## Section 1 — Recall & Recognition

**Q1.** The chapter describes two failure modes for startup naming. Which of the following correctly names both?

- A) Choosing a name that is too long, and choosing a name that is too generic
- B) Choosing a name that violates the archetype (primes wrong associations), and choosing a technically acceptable name that carries downstream costs (bar/lawyer/domain test failures)
- C) Choosing a name that is already taken, and choosing a name that is hard to pronounce

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter explicitly names two failure modes: (1) "a name that violates the archetype — a name that primes the wrong associations before the product has a chance to establish its own," and (2) "a name that is technically fine but carries downstream costs — it fails the bar test, fails the lawyer test, fails the domain test." Length (A) and trademark conflict (C) are subsets of the second failure mode, not the two structural categories.

**Q2.** The chapter distinguishes "earned meaning" from "borrowed meaning" in product naming. Which of the following correctly describes the Stripe model?

- A) Borrowed meaning — Stripe borrowed trust from the financial industry to signal reliability
- B) Earned meaning — the name was abstract at launch; the company trained the association between "Stripe" and payment APIs over years
- C) Both — Stripe used its founder reputation to borrow meaning while earning new associations

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "Earned meaning (the Stripe approach). The product name is abstract or minimal; the company spends years training the association between the name and the product category. Stripe did not mean payments before Stripe the company; now it does, in relevant circles." The name was meaningless before the company gave it meaning. Borrowed meaning (A, C) applies to extensions of an existing brand.

The consequence: developers who switched to Stripe did not feel they were betraying a prior affiliation; they felt they were upgrading their infrastructure." **Q4.** The chapter defines a Sage company's voice using Stripe as the primary example. Which set of descriptors does the chapter use?

- B) Precise, intellectually serious, slightly understated; signals the intended reader has technical depth

**Q5.** True or False: According to the chapter, a brand strategy document that spans two pages demonstrates greater strategic depth than a one-page document.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter is explicit: "A brand strategy document that is two pages has not yet been specified. It is still listing rather than deciding." The one-page constraint is "not a formatting preference. It is a specification test." Length signals insufficient decision-making: "Every section of a one-page document must be compressed to its essential commitment — the specific claim that constrains behavior, stripped of the explanatory prose that makes it comfortable but vague."

## Section 2 — Application & Analysis

**Q6.** A student is building a legal research AI tool and identifies her archetype as Sage. She is considering three candidate names: *JusticeMax*, *LexForm*, and *Arbor*. Apply the archetype-alignment pre-screening from the chapter to eliminate the weakest candidate and explain why.

- A) Eliminate *Arbor* — it has no legal connotation and will confuse the audience
- B) Eliminate *JusticeMax* — it primes Hero energy (competitive, maximizing) which contradicts the Sage archetype
- C) Eliminate *LexForm* — it is a domain-specific compound and will fail the bar test

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states Sage names "tend to be precise, slightly understated, often functional in their connotation" (examples: Stripe, Linear, Notion, Vercel). *JusticeMax* is energetic and action-forward — a Hero connotation. The chapter says names that "prime wrong-archetype associations are eliminated before the three tests." *Arbor* (A) is abstract, which is consistent with earned-meaning Sage naming. *LexForm* (C) may be testable for bar/domain issues but is not the archetype violation.

**Q7.** The chapter's coherence check requires testing four component pairs. One pair is Mission ↔ UVP. Using Stripe as the reference case, explain what this coherence check is testing and what a failure would look like.

**Model Answer:** The Mission ↔ UVP coherence check asks: does the UVP describe a way of doing what the mission says? For Stripe: the mission is "Increase the GDP of the internet" and the UVP is "the cleanest payment API, integrable in seven lines of code, with documentation that does not require a sales call to understand." Coherence holds because the UVP describes a specific mechanism — removing integration friction — that directly advances the mission of increasing digital economic activity.

A failure would look like a UVP that describes a product capability orthogonal to the mission's stated purpose. For example, a mission about "making financial infrastructure accessible globally" paired with a UVP about "the fastest settlement times for high-volume merchants" — the UVP serves an enterprise optimization goal that doesn't connect to global accessibility. When this pair is incoherent, the product builds features the mission wouldn't justify, and marketing makes promises the mission can't support.

**Q8.** The chapter says: "A brand becomes legible when an audience can predict what a company will and will not do." It then runs a thought experiment contrasting Company A (explicit no-list) and Company B (no no-list) after twelve months. Which outcome does the chapter predict for Company B's team?

- A) Company B's team is more innovative because it is unconstrained by a no-list
- B) Company B's team spends significant time in prioritization debates that a no-list would have resolved quickly
- C) Company B's team is more responsive to customer requests, which compounds into a stronger brand

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter's exact text: "Company B's team spends two days per week in prioritization debates that the no-list would have resolved in five minutes." The chapter frames the no-list not as a limitation but as "the mechanism that converts a set of product decisions into a recognizable brand." Lack of constraint (A) produces incoherence, not innovation; responsiveness (C) produces brand blur, not strength.

**Q9.** The chapter describes the Sage archetype's shadow as dogmatism and says "naming your shadow explicitly" is how you build monitoring for it. Using Stripe as the worked example, explain what this shadow-naming practice accomplishes strategically that a standard SWOT weakness analysis would not.

**Model Answer:** A SWOT weakness identifies things the company does poorly — gaps in capability or resources. Shadow-naming identifies a failure mode that is structurally produced by the company's strength, not its weakness. Stripe's API versioning policy (maintaining every API version indefinitely) is not a gap in capability — it is the cost of the Sage commitment to rigor and developer reliability. The chapter frames the shadow as "the cost of the strength."

Naming the shadow explicitly in the strategy document means the team is monitoring for the moment when a core commitment tips into its own failure mode. No SWOT analysis would flag "too rigorous" as a risk, because SWOT treats rigor as a strength. Shadow-naming treats over-rigor as a known risk to actively manage — different teams can monitor different shadow indicators (codebase complexity, iteration speed, feedback dismissal) without the failure mode being framed as weakness or incompetence.

## Section 3 — Synthesis & Evaluation

**Q10.** An investor reviews a startup's brand strategy document and notes that the values section lists "Customer Obsession" and "Speed" as core values. The no-list contains no entry that follows from either value. Using the chapter's coherence framework, evaluate what this gap reveals about the strategy document, and propose what a coherent no-list entry for each value would look like.

**Model Answer:** The chapter states: "If a value has no no-list consequences, it is not a value — it is a preference." The coherence check requires that every no-list entry can be traced to a value, and conversely, that every value produces at least one no-list entry. A values section with no no-list consequences means the values are decorative — they describe aspirations, not commitments. The chapter's test: name two specific decisions the company would make differently from a competitor with different values. If you can't, the value isn't specified.

For "Customer Obsession" to become a real value, a no-list entry might read: "We will not ship a feature until we have spoken directly with at least three customers who have used a prototype — even if the feature is on a tight deadline." This is specific, costly (delays shipping), and testable over time.

For "Speed" to become a real value, a no-list entry might read: "We will not hold a features-vs-roadmap planning meeting longer than 30 minutes — if a decision cannot be made in that window, the default is to ship and iterate." This is specific (30 minutes), archetype-consistent if the company is Hero-archetype oriented, costly (not all decisions are quick), and testable.

Without these entries, both values are claims the company cannot be held to — which means they do not constrain any decision.

**Q11.** A second-year student builds an AI-powered career coaching tool aimed at first-generation college students applying to graduate programs. She commits to the Caregiver archetype. Draft two entries for her startup's no-list that pass all four criteria (specific, archetype-consistent, costly, testable), and explain which part of the Stripe reverse-engineering method (from Part V) she should use to identify whether a well-funded competitor has already made opposite choices.

**Model Answer:** Two Caregiver-archetype no-list entries for this startup:

**Entry 1:** "We will not offer a premium tier that gives paying users faster response times or priority access to better model outputs — every student gets the same experience regardless of ability to pay." Specific: a new engineer knows what not to build. Caregiver-consistent: the Caregiver's core drive is anticipatory care; a pricing model that disadvantages the most resource-constrained students directly contradicts the archetype. Costly: a tiered model is a standard monetization path competitors at this stage use. Testable: two years from now, check whether a premium tier was added.

**Entry 2:** "We will not optimize for engagement metrics (session time, return frequency) — the product succeeds when the student no longer needs it, and we will not build features that create dependency." Specific: the engineering team knows not to add push notifications or streak mechanics. Caregiver-consistent: a Caregiver company that manufactured dependency would be expressing its shadow (enabling rather than empowering). Costly: engagement metrics drive investor confidence in the short term. Testable: check the analytics dashboard in two years for the absence of streak/retention features.

To identify competitor opposite choices: the chapter's reverse-engineering method (Part V §5.1) says to "write the no-list from the negative evidence" — specifically, look at "what product category has the company been asked to enter and declined? What customers have they publicly said are not their target?" She should read competitor pricing pages (for tier structures), onboarding flows (for engagement mechanics), and hiring pages (for growth-hacker vs. impact-focused roles). Competitor behavior that contradicts her no-list entries confirms the entries are genuinely costly choices, not free constraints.
