## Section 1 — Recall & Recognition

**Q1.** The chapter opens with the Pepsi 2008 redesign case. What does the leaked "BreathTaking" document illustrate about visual work done without strategic substance?

- A) It shows that professional branding agencies produce work that is too abstract for consumer audiences
- B) Without strategic substance to express, the visual work had to borrow meaning from external references (the Mona Lisa, the Golden Ratio, physics) because there was no internal anchor — producing a rationale that was incoherent and widely mocked
- C) It demonstrates that logo redesigns always damage brand equity and should be avoided

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "The brand had not committed to anything specific that the visual could express. The visual borrowed from everything, justified itself by association with everything, and ended up communicating nothing." The Pepsi case is used specifically to show what happens when visual work lacks the strategic substance it would otherwise express.

**Q2.** The chapter lists six components of a visual identity system. Which component does it describe as "the highest-frequency brand signal" — appearing on every pixel of every interface and every page of every document?

- A) Typography
- B) Color palette
- C) Logo and wordmark

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states explicitly: "Color is the highest-frequency brand signal. Every pixel of every interface carries the palette. Every page of every document. Every email header. A wrong color decision is encountered more often than any other wrong decision." Typography (A) and logo (C) are also load-bearing, but the frequency argument is made specifically about color.

**Q3.** True or False: According to the chapter, the WCAG AA accessibility standard requires a contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for all text, regardless of size.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter specifies two thresholds: "Normal text (below 18pt): contrast ratio of 4.5:1 minimum. Large text (18pt or above, or 14pt bold or above) and graphical objects: contrast ratio of 3:1 minimum." The standard is size-dependent. The chapter also includes a worked example showing caption text failing at 2.9:1 for normal-text size but passing for large text.

**Q4.** The chapter describes the Tropicana 2009 repackaging failure. Which of the following correctly identifies the structural problem?

- A) The new packaging used poor-quality photography that made the product look unappetizing
- B) The redesign expressed the wrong archetype — the original was Innocent (pure, simple, natural), while the new packaging expressed a Lover/Ruler premium positioning, which was good design but for the wrong brand
- C) The new packaging failed the accessibility test because the white-on-white color scheme lacked contrast

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "The visual was good; it was an excellent expression of an archetype that was not Tropicana's." The original orange-with-straw was Innocent archetype visual; the new packaging expressed Lover/Ruler premium positioning. "Sales dropped 20% in two months. The original packaging was restored." This is an archetype mismatch case, not a quality or accessibility failure.

**Q5.** True or False: According to the chapter, a wireframe that encodes a Sage archetype structurally should lead the home page's above-the-fold section with the most impressive project image.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter states the opposite: "A Sage wireframe has more text density, longer-form sections... The home page's above-the-fold has the positioning sentence prominently — the Sage leads with the thinking, not the image." Leading with a large project image is the Hero archetype pattern: "A Hero wireframe has a large above-the-fold image or video loop, a metrics strip."

## Section 2 — Application & Analysis

**Q6.** A student building a career coaching AI tool commits to the Caregiver archetype. She is selecting a typography pair. Which of the following selections is most archetype-aligned according to the chapter's guidance?

- A) Aktiv Grotesk Bold (display) + DM Sans Regular (body) — bold geometric sans-serifs that signal confidence and momentum
- B) Nunito (display) + Lato (body) — rounded sans-serifs that read as warm and approachable
- C) Source Serif 4 (display) + Inter (body) — classical serif that signals depth and authority

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "Caregiver archetypes lean toward rounded sans-serifs or warm humanist typefaces: Nunito, Mulish, Lato, Figtree... The combination signals: we are approachable and we are here for you." Aktiv Grotesk (A) is the Hero recommendation. Source Serif 4 + Inter (C) is the Sage recommendation.

**Q7.** The chapter says anti-references in a creative brief are "often more useful than references." Using the Sage-brand anti-reference example from the chapter, explain what work anti-references do that positive references cannot.

**Model Answer:** Positive references show what to aim for; they define the target. Anti-references make the negative space explicit — they define what the design must not become, and why. The chapter's example: "Red Bull's visual identity — saturated, diagonal, high-motion energy is exactly the archetypal mismatch we need to avoid. Our users want to trust the analysis; Hero-archetype visual language implies salesmanship, not rigor."

The chapter argues that anti-references constrain the design in a direction that positive references alone leave ambiguous. A designer given only "like Stripe's documentation site" might still drift toward saturated, energetic elements on the assumption that some dynamism is always better. An anti-reference that explicitly names the drift direction ("never Red Bull's energy") closes that space. Anti-references also encode the archetype's shadow risk — they name the failure mode the design needs to actively avoid, which a reference document can only imply.

**Q8.** The chapter's accessibility audit table shows caption text (#9CA3AF on #FAFAF8) failing the AA normal-text standard at a ratio of 2.9:1. The proposed fix darkens the caption to #6B7280, returning 4.6:1. What principle does this fix illustrate about the relationship between accessibility compliance and palette character?

- A) Accessibility requirements always require replacing the original palette color with a fundamentally different hue
- B) Small adjustments in value (lightness) can bring a failing combination into compliance without changing the palette's visual character — the hue and identity remain the same
- C) Caption text should never be tested against AA standards because it is not primary content

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "Adjusting usually means darkening the text color or lightening the background — small shifts in value (the V in HSV) can bring a failing combination into compliance without changing the palette's visual character." The fix demonstrates that accessibility and aesthetic coherence are not opposed — compliant adjustments are fine-tuned within the existing palette logic.

**Q9.** The chapter's "Feynman test" for visual identity asks a specific question about any design decision. State the Feynman test as the chapter defines it, and explain what it detects.

**Model Answer:** The Feynman test from the chapter: "Can you pick up any single design decision from your visual system — a color choice, a typeface, a layout element — and explain in two sentences why it is there, what archetype it expresses, and what it would have to be replaced with if you switched archetypes? If yes, the system is understood. If not, a decision slipped through on preference rather than strategy."

The test detects the difference between decisions made strategically (grounded in archetype, traceable to the creative brief) and decisions made by preference (chosen because they look good, but not anchored to the brand's strategic commitments). A decision that passes the Feynman test has an answer to all three questions. A decision that fails it — where the designer cannot explain the archetype it expresses — was made by taste, not by strategy. The chapter's broader argument is that preference-driven decisions produce the Pepsi outcome: visual work that looks assembled rather than coherent, because nothing is tying the components to the same strategic anchor.

## Section 3 — Synthesis & Evaluation

**Q10.** The chapter describes the Yahoo 2013 "30-day logo journey" as a "strategy-free process." A classmate argues: "Showing 30 concepts publicly was actually smart — it crowdsourced feedback and let the audience co-design the brand." Using the chapter's framework, construct a counter-argument that identifies what the 30-day process revealed about Yahoo's brand strategy, and why crowdsourced feedback cannot substitute for strategic substance.

**Model Answer:** The chapter's counter-argument: by publishing 30 daily concepts that were not consistent with each other, Yahoo "demonstrated that no archetype was constraining the final choice." Crowdsourcing feedback from an audience that includes people with contradictory preferences does not produce strategic coherence — it produces committee output. The final logo "read as committee output — which it was."

The structural problem is that crowdsourced feedback evaluates individual visual choices against individual preferences, not against a strategic archetype that the company has committed to. A Sage company asking the public which of 30 logos they prefer will receive votes that reflect the audience's aesthetic preferences, not the brand's strategic commitments. The 30-day process substituted public process for private strategy — the question was "which logo do people like?" when the question should have been "which logo expresses the Sage/Hero/Rebel commitment Yahoo has actually made?" Without the strategic answer, no amount of public feedback can produce an anchored choice.

The chapter's outcome illustrates the consequence: "Yahoo's visual identity never stabilized because the strategic substance required to anchor a visual identity was absent." A year later the company was in decline; in 2017 it was sold for under $5 billion from a peak valuation over $100 billion.

**Q11.** A student builds a legal AI research tool and commits to the Sage archetype. She has produced a color palette and is now selecting a wireframe structure. She proposes leading the home page above-the-fold with a metrics strip showing "500K+ legal documents processed" and a bold CTA button. Apply the chapter's archetypal wireframe logic to evaluate this choice. If it is misaligned, propose an alternative above-the-fold section that is Sage-consistent, and explain what the Sage homepage is trying to accomplish that her current version contradicts.

**Model Answer:** The proposed above-the-fold section is misaligned. A metrics strip with a quantitative volume claim and a bold CTA is Hero-archetype structure — it "implies momentum" and positions achievement (volume processed) as the primary signal of trust. The Hero homepage says: we are fast, we have done a lot, take action now. The chapter states: "A Hero wireframe has a large above-the-fold image or video loop, a metrics strip (performance numbers displayed boldly), short and punchy section copy."

A Sage-consistent alternative: the above-the-fold should lead with a positioning sentence that communicates depth and precision — for example: "Legal research that cites what it finds and flags what it does not know." This positions the tool around rigor and honesty (Sage voice: "willing to say 'we don't know yet'") rather than volume or speed. The CTA should not be a bold action button; it should be a secondary navigation: "Read how it works" or "See a sample analysis." The Sage homepage leads with thinking, not achievement.

What the Sage homepage is trying to accomplish: the chapter says the Sage palette and wireframe "signal 'we have thought about this carefully.'" The developer/legal-professional audience for this tool, per the chapter's Figure 8.5, "distrusts marketing-speak, vague claims" and "rewards demonstrated depth." Leading with a metrics strip signals volume, not depth. A positioning sentence signals that the brand has committed to a specific claim about how it works — which is what the Sage audience actually evaluates before trusting the tool.
