## Section 1 — Recall & Recognition

**Q1.** The chapter describes Donald Miller's Customer-as-Hero framework. Which role does the chapter say the brand should occupy in a customer story?

- A) The hero — the brand overcomes the obstacles and achieves the goal
- B) The guide — the brand provides clarity, a plan, and tools; the customer takes action and is transformed
- C) The threshold guardian — the brand sets the conditions the customer must meet before receiving help

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "In a customer story, the customer is always the hero. The brand is never the hero. The brand is the guide — Yoda to the customer's Luke Skywalker, Gandalf to the customer's Frodo." The guide role is structural in Miller's framework: "the brand has a plan; the customer faces a problem; the brand's guidance helps the customer take action; the customer is transformed."

**Q2.** The Pepsi *Live for Now* 2017 ad activated three narrative-archetype mismatch failure pathways simultaneously. Which of the following correctly identifies Pepsi's actual archetype and the story shape the ad attempted?

- A) Pepsi's actual archetype: Rebel. Attempted story shape: Caregiver service story
- B) Pepsi's actual archetype: Innocent (with Jester elements). Attempted story shape: Hero's Journey, with the brand as the magical resolution device (a Magician move)
- C) Pepsi's actual archetype: Everyman. Attempted story shape: Transformation story

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "Pepsi's archetype is Innocent with Jester elements... The *Live for Now* ad attempted a Hero's Journey at the customer level... The brand was cast as the magical object that resolves the conflict." The chapter names this "the first failure: casting the brand as a magical resolution device is a Magician move, not an Innocent move."

**Q3.** True or False: According to the chapter, the Madison Intelligence Agent README is described as Sage brand storytelling because it dramatizes the founders' journey of struggle and success.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter explicitly describes the README as Sage storytelling through a different mechanism: it opens with throughput numbers (870 articles/day, 90% deduplication, sub-3-minute latency), walks through the five-layer architecture, and explains design decisions. The chapter says "the mechanism *is* the story" for Sage storytelling — "the Sage does not dramatize triumph; the Sage demonstrates understanding." The README teaches; it does not dramatize.

**Q4.** The chapter distinguishes between Campbell's Hero's Journey and Booker's Quest in terms of what each centers. Which of the following is correct?

- A) Campbell centers the personal transformation of the hero; Booker's Quest centers the importance of the objective itself
- B) Campbell centers the audience's identification with the brand; Booker centers the competitive landscape the brand must overcome
- C) Both frameworks center the hero's transformation; they differ only in the number of stages

**Answer:** A
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "The Quest differs from the Hero's Journey in emphasis: the Journey centers the personal transformation of the hero; the Quest centers the importance of the objective itself. A brand on a Quest story is making a claim about *why the destination matters*, not just who is traveling."

---

**Q5.** True or False: According to the chapter, a Jester archetype can effectively tell a sincere Service story when the product genuinely serves people in need.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter states directly: "A Jester telling a sincere Service story will read as insincere." The constraint is structural: "Story shapes that require a different archetype's commitments will misfire." The Jester derives power from inversion and unexpected angles — a sincere service frame requires the Caregiver's register. Adopting it would read as imposed, not authentic, regardless of the brand's genuine intent.

## Section 2 — Application & Analysis

**Q6.** A Sage archetype building a technical AI tool wants to publish its first piece of content. Using the chapter's archetype-to-channel matching, which combination is most native to the Sage?

- A) A viral Twitter/X thread with short, punchy observations about the AI industry
- B) A GitHub README opening with mechanism and quantified outputs, plus a LinkedIn article presenting a specific argument about an aspect of the field
- C) An Instagram reel showing behind-the-scenes product development

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "GitHub README and documentation: underused as brand storytelling. For Sage archetypes, the README is often the first thing a potential collaborator or customer sees. A README that opens with mechanism and quantified outputs is Sage storytelling at its best." The chapter also states: "LinkedIn articles... Sage and Hero archetypes thrive here." Twitter/X (A) is described as "optional, archetype-dependent" — better suited to Rebel and Jester. Visual/behind-the-scenes formats (C) are not aligned to the Sage's evidence-forward, teaching-oriented storytelling mode.

**Q7.** Apply the chapter's three-question structural test for customer-as-hero to the following draft case study opening: "I built a pipeline that automatically aggregates competitor news daily using n8n. The tool was built using GPT-4o-mini for sentiment scoring. It outputs a Markdown report." Identify which elements are present, which are missing, and what the writer should do to meet the test's requirements.

**Model Answer:** The three-question test asks: (1) Who is the protagonist in the first paragraph? (2) What does the customer risk or struggle with? (3) What specifically changed?

This draft fails all three checks. (1) The protagonist in the first paragraph is "I" — the builder, not the customer. The test says: "If the answer is 'me' or 'my tool' or 'my company,' the story is mis-oriented." (2) No customer risk or struggle is named — there is no problem, no stakes, no difficulty. "A pipeline that automatically aggregates" describes a capability, not a pain. (3) No transformation is described — the report exists, but we do not know who reads it, what they could not do before, or what changed.

The revision should open with a customer moment: who was struggling, what they were doing, how often, and what the cost was. A version that passes: "Marketing managers at mid-size B2B companies were spending four hours per week manually pulling competitor news across six browser tabs. The output was stale by the time it reached the team and inconsistent week to week. After deploying the pipeline, that four hours dropped to ten minutes. The reports arrive daily in Slack, already scored for sentiment and ranked by relevance." All three checks pass: customer is the protagonist, stakes are real (four hours/week, inconsistency), transformation is specific (four hours to ten minutes).

**Q8.** The chapter names three failure pathways for narrative-archetype mismatch. The Bud Light/Dylan Mulvaney case is described primarily as which pathway?

- A) Pathway 1 — the story shape requires emotional registers the archetype cannot deliver authentically
- B) Pathway 2 — the story positions the brand in a role inconsistent with its archetype (Everyman attempting a Magician transformation story without Magician foundation)
- C) Pathway 3 — the story's cultural content is accessible only to archetypes the brand does not inhabit

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter describes the Bud Light case explicitly in terms of Pathway 2: "Everyman brands signal *belonging to the mainstream*... The Magician archetype's transformation story requires a foundation of demonstrated vision and earned trust in the brand's capacity to transform. Bud Light had built no such foundation." The brand was positioned in the Magician role — marking transformation, signaling openness to expanding who "belongs" — without the Magician's brand history to support that positioning.

**Q9.** The chapter describes a six-element case study template. Two of the elements — "what they tried before" and "the meta-lesson" — are described as frequently skipped. Explain what structural function each one performs that the other five elements cannot replace.

**Model Answer:** "What they tried before" establishes why the approach in the case study was necessary rather than obvious. Without it, the reader may conclude that the customer simply had not tried to solve their problem yet — and that any reasonable solution would have worked. The chapter says: "This is often the most skipped element and the most important — without it, the reader does not understand why your approach was necessary rather than obvious." The structural function is to create the space for the approach to matter: "They tried X but it did not work because Y" is what makes the tool's specific design defensible.

"The meta-lesson" provides the teaching that makes the case study worth reading for people who are not the customer in the story. The chapter says: "not just 'our tool works' — what does this case teach about the problem domain, the approach, the kind of transformation that is possible?" A case study without a meta-lesson is private evidence for the specific customer who lived it. A case study with a meta-lesson is transferable knowledge for anyone in the field who recognizes the problem type. This is the element that makes a case study sharable and citable beyond the immediate audience.

## Section 3 — Synthesis & Evaluation

**Q10.** The Jaguar "Copy Nothing" rebrand (November 2024) is analyzed in the chapter as a case of "narrative-substance mismatch." The chapter notes the rebrand was "broadly negative" in coverage and was cited as a "textbook example of narrative-substance mismatch." A Jaguar communications director argues: "The coverage was negative because journalists and automotive fans are conservative — they resist all disruption. The rebrand was ahead of its time." Using the chapter's mismatch framework, construct a counter-argument that identifies the specific structural failure, independent of audience conservatism.

**Model Answer:** The chapter's counter-argument does not depend on audience conservatism. The structural failure has two independent components:

First, the archetype-role mismatch: "Ruler brands derive their authority from their relationship to existing excellence — the standard others aspire to. Outlaw brands derive their energy from explicitly rejecting that standard. A Ruler attempting an Outlaw story is not rejecting convention; it is abandoning the specific convention (excellence, prestige, earned authority) that constituted its value." This failure applies regardless of how progressive or conservative the audience is. A Ruler brand that signals "Copy Nothing" is not transgressing against external authority — it is undermining the internal authority structure that made it valuable to its customers. A truly conservative audience would reject it for this reason; a truly progressive audience would reject it because Jaguar has no history of Outlaw cultural content.

Second, the narrative-substance mismatch: "The Magician's transformation story requires that the impossible thing actually becomes possible — the mystery is followed by a revelation. Without the cars, the story had no second act." The rebrand launched with no cars to anchor it. A transformation story with no transformation is not ahead of its time; it is structurally incomplete. The audience recognized that the story's second act was missing, not because they were conservative but because the story had promised something it had not yet delivered. Audience conservatism cannot explain the structural gap — it existed independently of who was watching.

**Q11.** A student building a Sage-archetype AI legal research tool wants to publish her first three pieces of content: an origin story, a case study, and a thought-leadership piece. Using the chapter's guidance on archetype-native story shapes, cadence, and the three-check diagnostic, design a specific content plan that passes the archetype check for each piece. Include: the story shape, the appropriate channel, a one-sentence thesis or frame for each piece, and one sentence on why each choice is archetype-consistent.

**Model Answer:**

**Piece 1 — Origin story (300–500 words, published on the portfolio site and LinkedIn)**
Story shape: Methodology story (Sage-native). Frame: "I spent three months watching lawyers manually re-read the same statutes in different cases, not because they were inefficient but because no tool could tell them which prior decisions were actually relevant to their specific jurisdiction and fact pattern. Here is what I built, and why the architecture reflects what I learned about the problem." Channel: portfolio site's About page + LinkedIn article. Archetype-consistent: The Sage origin story is a story of investigation, not of heroic struggle — it centers the problem's mechanism and what the investigation produced. The chapter says: "A Sage archetype tells an origin story as a Methodology story — here is the problem I was trying to solve, here is how I came to understand it more precisely, here is what the investigation produced."

**Piece 2 — Case study (800–1,800 words, published on the portfolio site and LinkedIn)**
Story shape: Service/Transformation story with customer as hero (Miller framework applied to a Methodology-grounded case). Frame: "A solo immigration attorney was reading three-to-five prior decisions per new case to build jurisdictional context. After using the tool, that reading dropped to one — the system surfaced the two most legally relevant decisions and flagged the three she could deprioritize with a confidence score. Time per case prep: down from ninety minutes to forty." Channel: portfolio case study page + LinkedIn. Archetype-consistent: the chapter says Sage case studies demonstrate mechanism and quantified outputs; this framing opens with the customer's specific problem and closes with specific metrics, both Sage-aligned. The honesty about limits (confidence scores, not certainty) is also Sage voice.

**Piece 3 — Thought-leadership piece (800–1,500 words, published on LinkedIn + optional Substack)**
Story shape: Quest/Methodology argument. Thesis: "Most legal AI tools are measuring retrieval accuracy. They should be measuring jurisdictional relevance — these are different things, and conflating them is producing tools that feel accurate but fail in practice." Channel: LinkedIn article (the chapter says "Sage and Hero archetypes thrive here" for thought-leadership). Archetype-consistent: the chapter defines thought-leadership as "a published argument about something your field gets wrong... specific enough that a hostile reader can push back on it." This thesis is falsifiable — a hostile reader could argue that retrieval accuracy is a proxy for jurisdictional relevance and cite evidence. Cadence: one piece per month is the chapter's recommended sustainable pace; this three-piece plan spans a quarter, which is within the sustainable range.
