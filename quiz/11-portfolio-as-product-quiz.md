## Section 1 — Recall & Recognition

**Q1.** The chapter describes three compounding channels for a portfolio. Which channel does it identify as the one "most graduates design for" — while the other two contain "where the compounding lives"?

- A) Template effects — when others fork or clone your design
- B) Direct hiring — a recruiter finds your portfolio and advances you to the next stage
- C) Indirect reference — someone bookmarks your portfolio and mentions your name months later

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "Channel 1: Direct hiring... This is the channel every graduate thinks about. It is also the most visible and the most predictable." It then says: "Channel 2 and Channel 3 are where the compounding lives." Indirect reference (C) and template effects (A) are described as the under-optimized channels with higher long-term returns.

**Q2.** The chapter uses Brittany Chiang's portfolio as the primary example of all three compounding channels simultaneously. Which facts does the chapter cite to illustrate the template effects channel?

- A) Chiang's portfolio was featured in a "best portfolios" article, which drove her to Apple
- B) The GitHub repo for her v4 portfolio site has been forked over 6,000 times and starred over 9,000 as of 2024 — generations of developers used her design as the foundation of their first portfolio
- C) Chiang published quarterly case studies that attracted 10,000 monthly readers

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states exactly: "The GitHub repo for v4 of her site has been forked over 6,000 times and starred over 9,000 as of 2024. Generations of developers used Chiang's design as the foundation of their first portfolio." This is the template effects channel — the design becomes a starting point for others, each derivative carrying her name forward.

**Q3.** True or False: According to the chapter, a portfolio with ten projects showing breadth is stronger than a portfolio with three to six curated projects, because more evidence of capability is always better.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "Most graduates make the same curation mistake: they include too many projects... The recruiter sees ten small things instead of three large things. The portfolio reads as 'I have done things' rather than 'I am this kind of engineer.'" Three to six projects is the recommended range: "Three is better than six if the three are excellent and the six would include mediocre work."

**Q4.** The chapter describes the case study for the AI tool as needing to perform four functions simultaneously. Which function does it say "most engineering case studies omit entirely"?

- A) Frame the problem with the customer-as-hero opening
- B) Connect the technical work to the brand — why the interface is designed the way it is, what the $100,000 no was, what the alignment audit revealed

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "A note on archetype, voice, and visual choices... This is the section most engineering case studies omit entirely. ## Section 2 — Application & Analysis

**Q6.** A student has six projects in her portfolio: a data pipeline for a retail client, a sentiment analysis tool for social media, a React dashboard, a course assignment on Kubernetes, a recommendation engine for e-commerce, and her Madison-pattern AI intelligence agent. Her archetype is Sage and her positioning is "AI engineer building developer-first intelligence tools." Using the negative-space rule from the chapter, identify which projects to include and which to exclude, with one-sentence justifications.

- A) Include all six — maximum evidence of technical range signals versatility to a broad audience
- B) Include the Madison agent, the data pipeline, the sentiment analysis tool, and the recommendation engine; exclude the React dashboard and the Kubernetes course assignment — the excluded items don't reinforce the "intelligence tools" positioning, and course assignments in particular signal student rather than builder
- C) Include only the Madison agent — it is the strongest single project and everything else dilutes the signal

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "The selection criteria are: which three to six projects, combined, make the strongest possible case for the archetype and the positioning claim?" The Madison agent, data pipeline, sentiment tool, and recommendation engine all demonstrate intelligence-building relevant to the Sage AI engineer positioning. The React dashboard is general web development ("not projects that demonstrate general-purpose web development"), and the Kubernetes assignment is course work rather than a produced artifact. Answer A contradicts the chapter's core curation argument; Answer C goes below the minimum range for evidencing a positioning claim.

**Q7.** The chapter distinguishes between "portfolio-as-product" and "portfolio-as-resume-with-images." Using the chapter's five-artifact framework, explain what distinguishes the fifth artifact (portfolio-as-product) from the second artifact (portfolio website), and what work must be done to achieve convergence.

**Model Answer:** The chapter's second artifact (portfolio website) is "a designed presentation of selected work, optimized for impression-building — a recruiter or hiring manager who has already passed the filter." It is a standalone surface. The fifth artifact (portfolio-as-product) is the convergence state: "a coherent brand artifact in which artifacts 1 [resume], 2 [portfolio website], and 4 [LinkedIn] tell the same story from three angles — same archetype, same voice, same selected projects, same positioning sentence — while serving their different reading speeds and audiences."

The distinction is that portfolio-as-product requires alignment across surfaces, not just quality on one surface. A well-designed portfolio website that uses different projects, a different voice, or a different positioning claim than the resume or LinkedIn is still portfolio-as-resume-with-images — three separate presentations, not one coherent product.

The work required for convergence: (1) the same positioning sentence on the portfolio above-the-fold and the LinkedIn headline, (2) the same archetype legible across all three surfaces, (3) the same three to five projects featured across portfolio, LinkedIn featured section, and resume project entries, (4) voice consistency — the portfolio about section and the LinkedIn about section should sound like the same person when read aloud back to back.

**Q8.** The chapter names two tradeoffs for v0 versus hand-coded React as portfolio building tools. A student argues: "I should hand-code my portfolio from scratch because it will demonstrate stronger React skills." What does the chapter say about this trade-off, and when does it recommend which approach?

- A) The chapter agrees — hand-coding always produces the strongest technical signal
- B) The chapter says hand-coding gives full control and a strong technical signal to React-oriented engineering teams, but for most students with a course deadline, v0 is the right trade — the distinction comes from brand strategy and content, not from the tool
- C) The chapter recommends v0 for all students because hand-coded portfolios are too slow to load

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "The advantage [of hand-coding]: full control, the most distinctive output, and the strongest technical signal to a React-oriented engineering team. The disadvantage: time... For a course with a deadline, v0 is the right trade for most students." On v0's limitation: "The tool is a starting point. Your Chapter 8, 9, and 10 work is the ending point" — distinction comes from brand strategy and content, not from whether the code was generated or written by hand.

**Q9.** The coherence audit requires sending portfolio URL, LinkedIn URL, and resume to one person with a single question. What is that question, and what specific elements does the chapter say to check if the answer is "no"?

**Model Answer:** The question the chapter specifies: "does this feel like the same person from three angles?"

If the answer is no, the chapter lists four specific elements to check:
1. Does the positioning sentence on the portfolio above-the-fold match the LinkedIn headline?
2. Is the archetype legible in all three surfaces? (A Sage archetype should read as insight-forward in all three; a Creator should read as output-forward.)
3. Are the same three to five projects featured across the portfolio project section, the LinkedIn featured section, and the resume's project entries?
4. Is the voice consistent? Read the portfolio about section and the LinkedIn about section aloud back to back — they should sound like the same person.

The chapter specifies the goal of the audit: "not making the three surfaces identical" but ensuring they "feel like three angles on one person rather than three separate presentations of three different candidates."

## Section 3 — Synthesis & Evaluation

**Q10.** A hiring manager reviews two portfolios. Portfolio A has eight projects presented as equal-weight tiles, each with a screenshot and two sentences. Portfolio B has four projects: one flagship AI tool presented as a 1,200-word case study with architecture diagram, metrics, and a brand-connection section, plus three supporting projects presented as shorter tiles. Portfolio A has technically harder projects; Portfolio B's centerpiece is less technically complex. Using the chapter's compounding framework, evaluate which portfolio is more likely to perform across all three channels, and identify the specific mechanism that explains the difference.

**Model Answer:** Portfolio B is more likely to perform across all three channels, and the explanation is the chapter's compounding mechanism.

Channel 1 (direct hiring): Both portfolios get through the filter. Portfolio A's harder projects may score slightly better on initial technical screening. But at the human-read stage, the chapter says "the recruiter sees ten small things instead of three large things" — A's eight equal-weight tiles read as "I have done things," not "I am this kind of engineer." Portfolio B's flagship case study is "the artifact that a hiring manager who has passed the filter and wants depth will read before deciding whether to move you forward" — it performs all four case study functions and gives the human reader something to evaluate.

Channel 2 (indirect reference): The chapter says the portfolio that performs here is "worth referencing — distinctive, coherent, executing with craft." Portfolio A is adequate and indistinguishable from similar portfolios. Portfolio B is structured around an argument — the flagship case study has a specific problem frame, technical work shown at depth, and brand connection explicit. Someone who encounters it and thinks "this is how to write a case study" or "this person thinks about engineering as design" will remember it and share it. Portfolio A produces no such reaction.

Channel 3 (template effects): Portfolio B's case study structure and flagship-plus-shelf architecture is the thing other students will borrow. The chapter's cohort analysis confirms this: "the portfolios that argued one flagship-plus-shelf relationship read as designed products."

The specific mechanism: the chapter's negative-space rule applied to curation. Portfolio B made an explicit exclusion decision — it does not include every project. The deliberate exclusion is what makes the included projects readable as a coherent argument rather than an inventory.

**Q11.** The chapter quotes from the Spring 2026 cohort: Chapter 21's portfolio included an embedded "Ask Guna AI" chatbot that let recruiters query the brand's vocabulary in real time, and Chapter 22's portfolio used a live-terminal hero rendering with CLI-prompt headers. Apply the chapter's four case study functions and the archetype-alignment principle to evaluate what these design choices accomplish strategically that a standard project-tile portfolio cannot. Then identify which archetype each choice is most likely aligned to and why.

**Model Answer:** Both choices demonstrate the chapter's "Function 3: Connect to the brand" — the case study function most engineering portfolios omit. But they do it at the portfolio level rather than within a case study.

The "Ask Guna AI" chatbot (Chapter 21) demonstrates the AI Integration pillar as "interactive proof rather than static project tile" — the chapter's phrase. Standard project-tile portfolios describe capabilities; this one lets the recruiter experience the capability in real time. This performs across all three compounding channels: Channel 1 (a recruiter can ask the chatbot a question and get an archetype-consistent response), Channel 2 (someone who encounters it shares it as "a portfolio that actually does something"), Channel 3 (other students borrow the embedded-AI pattern). The archetype most likely aligned: Sage. The chatbot answers questions about the brand's vocabulary — it is a teaching tool, patient and evidence-responsive, which is how Sage brands signal competence. Alternatively it could be Creator, since the chatbot is an artifact of creative engineering.

The live-terminal hero (Chapter 22) encodes the archetype structurally, which is the chapter's wireframe principle applied to the portfolio itself. The chapter says: "A Sage wireframe leads with the positioning sentence; a Hero wireframe leads with a project image." A terminal rendering leads with the artifact of engineering craft — the chapter describes it as "the archetype rendered as documentation page, not as marketing landing page." This makes the archetype legible before a single word is read, which is the highest-leverage brand signal. Most aligned archetype: Sage with Creator elements — the terminal aesthetic signals production-engineering depth (Sage) while the deliberate craft of the visual design signals that the engineer treats the interface as a designed artifact (Creator). The chapter's cohort analysis confirms: "Karthik's terminal aesthetic filters toward production-engineering hiring panels" — a specific audience filter, not a general aesthetic preference.

Both choices perform the chapter's "portfolio-as-product" distinction at the deepest level: they are portfolios that are themselves AI-tool demonstrations, making the case study and the proof of concept the same artifact.
