## Section 1 — Recall & Recognition

**Q1.** The chapter uses the Airbnb 2009 YC pitch deck as a worked example of the coherence principle. Which of the following correctly identifies the three properties the chapter says made the deck work?

- A) Beautiful design, comprehensive feature roadmap, and a large total addressable market
- B) Argument coherence (every slide reinforced one claim), specific numbers (real beta data), and team weight (named founders with relevant history)
- C) Memorable brand name, celebrity endorsement, and a viral product demo

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter explicitly names three properties: "Every slide answered the same question" (argument coherence), "Specific numbers did load-bearing work" (87 bookings in the first six weeks), and "The team slide carried weight" (three founders, named, with relevant work history). The chapter says: "These three properties — argument coherence, specific numbers, team weight — are not pitch-deck properties. They are *presentation* properties."

**Q3.** True or False: According to the chapter, a LinkedIn headline formatted as "Software Engineer at X" is preferred because it clearly communicates your current role to recruiters who are searching by job title.

**Answer:** False
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "*'AI Engineer at Acme Corp'* is a job title. *'AI engineer building tools that help knowledge workers reclaim their attention'* is a positioning sentence. **Q4.** The 10/20/30 rule for the final presentation comes from Guy Kawasaki. What does the chapter say the 30-point font constraint forces?

- A) Larger slides that are easier to read from the back of the room
- B) Editing — a presenter who cannot fit content at 30-point font has too much content per slide and is likely to read from the slides rather than present them
- C) Fewer slides, which reduces the presentation to ten slides maximum

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "His argument: a presenter who uses font smaller than 30 points has too much content per slide and is reading the slides rather than presenting them. Forcing 30-point font forces editing. Editing is what good presentations need." The constraint is explicitly about the discipline of compression, not legibility distance.

## Section 2 — Application & Analysis

**Q6.** The chapter contrasts two resume bullets for the same work: "Worked with the team on data engineering tasks" versus "Built a data pipeline ingesting 870 articles per day with a 90% deduplication rate, reducing manual curation time by four hours per week." Using the chapter's specific criteria, identify the three distinct elements that make the second bullet meet the "outcome statement" standard.

- A) The second bullet is longer and uses more professional vocabulary
- B) The second bullet contains a throughput number (870 articles), a quality metric (90% deduplication), and a business impact (four hours per week reduced) — making the claim specific, picturable, and auditable by a reviewer
- C) The second bullet mentions specific tools (n8n, pipeline) that score higher in ATS keyword matching

**Answer:** B
**Rationale:** The chapter states: "The outcome statement is specific. It contains a number (870 articles), a quality metric (90%), and a business impact (four hours per week)." And: "A reviewer reading it can picture what was built, at what scale, and what it was worth." The chapter's criterion is: "show outcomes, not responsibilities" — the outcome statement answers "what was built and what happened because I built it."

**Q7.** Slide 2 of the final presentation is the Problem slide. The chapter provides a worked example of two different problem framings. Using the chapter's guidance, explain what the "scene opening" problem slide does that the "average time" problem slide does not, and write a scene-opening problem slide for a legal research AI tool.

**Model Answer:** The chapter's worked example contrasts "Marketing managers spend an average of 4.2 hours per week manually curating competitive intelligence that is out of date before they use it" with a generic version ("knowledge workers spend too much time on manual tasks"). The specific version "lands harder" because it names who hurts, how often, and by how much.

But the chapter's higher-standard form is the scene opening from the Part 4 example: "It is 7:45 on a Tuesday morning. A marketing manager at a mid-sized B2B company opens her laptop. The first thing she does is check what happened in her competitor landscape overnight — and it takes her forty-five minutes of tab-switching across six dashboards." The scene opening puts the audience *inside* the problem before stating any statistics. The chapter says: "Open in scene, not in introduction. The first sixty seconds are when the audience decides whether to pay attention."

Scene opening for a legal research AI tool: "It is 9:15 on a Thursday morning. An immigration attorney in Boston receives a new case — a client who has been in the country for twelve years and has three prior legal proceedings on file. Before she can assess strategy, she needs to read the relevant statutes, find the controlling precedents in the First Circuit, and check for any recent rulings that would change the analysis. Two hours later, she has three tabs open and two questions she still cannot answer. The case appointment is in four hours. My tool does that research in eleven minutes."

That version names one person, one morning, one problem, with a specific time cost and a specific resolution.

**Q9.** The chapter describes Slide 9 ("You as the team") as "the most important slide in the deck for a hiring or partnership audience." What three specific elements does the chapter say this slide should contain, and why does the chapter describe this slide as doing "most of the persuasion work"?

**Model Answer:** The chapter lists the three elements for Slide 9: "Your archetype. Your methodology. The four-verb framework applied to your own work history. The portfolio URL on the slide."

The chapter says this slide does "most of the persuasion work" because at seed stage or hiring stage with limited track record, "the team slide is the pitch. Everything else is context for the question: do we believe these people can build this?" Applied to a student presenting: the audience has seen what was built (slides 3-5), the brand (slide 6), and the validation (slide 7). Slide 9 answers the underlying question beneath all of those: is this person the kind of builder who can sustain this work and grow with it? The archetype reveals how this person makes decisions. The methodology reveals how they think about building. The four-verb framework (Ideate/Build/Brand/Ship) demonstrates that they have moved through the full creative engineer arc — not just built, but positioned and shipped. The portfolio URL gives the audience a way to verify all claims immediately.

## Section 3 — Synthesis & Evaluation

**Q10.** The chapter ends with: "The Spence signaling argument from Chapter 1 closes here." Reconstruct the full chain connecting Chapter 1's Spence signaling mechanism to Chapter 12's final presentation. Explain specifically which signals produced in the course are "costly signals that AI tooling did not make cheap" and why they satisfy the Spence criterion.

**Model Answer:** Chapter 1 introduced Michael Spence's signaling mechanism: in markets where quality is hard to observe directly, a costly signal functions to separate high-quality candidates from lower-quality ones. The signal's cost must be positively correlated with the underlying quality being signaled — otherwise, it would be rational for anyone to acquire the signal regardless of quality, making it uninformative.

The chapter closes this loop in Chapter 12 by identifying which signals satisfy this criterion in the AI-augmented market. The chapter states: "the signals that separated you from other candidates — not the GitHub repository, but the deployed tool, the positioned brand, the published writing, the coherent presentation — are the costly signals that AI tooling did not make cheap."

Why each satisfies the Spence criterion:
- **Deployed AI tool**: requires PRD discipline (Chapter 4), pipeline architecture decisions under constraint (Chapter 5), agent specification and anti-hallucination patterns (Chapter 6), and interface alignment audit (Chapter 7). AI tooling assists each step but does not eliminate the decision-making cost. A low-quality candidate who attempts this without the underlying judgment produces an incoherent tool.
- **Positioned brand strategy**: the one-page document with archetype, negative-space list, and coherent component pairs requires the judgment to make real commitments. Generating text with AI is cheap; making specific, costly, testable commitments is not.
- **Published writing with a falsifiable thesis**: requires domain knowledge specific enough to take a position a hostile reader can push back on. Generic AI-generated content is cheap; an argument that is wrong in an interesting way is evidence of actual engagement with the domain.
- **Coherent presentation**: the 10-slide argument coherence test — "each slide leans on the previous one" — requires that all prior artifacts (tool, brand, narrative, portfolio) are internally consistent. AI tooling can generate individual slides but cannot generate the coherence that comes from having actually made all the prior commitments in sequence.

The full four-verb arc (Ideate → Build → Brand → Ship) is itself the costly signal: completing it in an archetype-aligned, internally-consistent way over one course takes sustained judgment, not just one well-prompted AI session.

**Q11.** The chapter argues that "Coherence beats polish." A student preparing for her final presentation has ten days remaining. She can spend those ten days: (Option A) redesigning her pitch deck to match a polished template she found online, adding animations and improving visual design; or (Option B) running the coherence audit across her resume, LinkedIn, portfolio, and deck — ensuring all four surfaces tell the same story from the same archetype with the same positioning sentence. Using the chapter's Airbnb example, the coherence principle, and the brand-coherence surface stack, make the case for Option B and identify the specific compounding risk of Option A.

**Model Answer:** The chapter's Airbnb example is the central evidence: "The deck raised $600,000 total. Not because of design. Because every slide reinforced the same argument." The chapter states explicitly: "The Airbnb deck that raised $600,000 in 2009 was not famous for design. It was famous because every slide reinforced the same argument. That is the standard."

Option A (polished template) adds production value to one surface without ensuring that surface says the same thing as the others. The chapter's coherence principle applied: "a presentation that contradicts itself, relies on vague claims, or buries the most important thing leaves a durable negative impression." A polished deck that is incoherent with the portfolio or the LinkedIn headline is worse than a plain deck that is coherent — the mismatch signals that the candidate manages surfaces independently rather than as a unified brand. This is the opposite of what the course work demonstrates.

The compounding risk of Option A: the final presentation is described as "the densest brand impression surface that exists." A recruiter or hiring audience who encounters a polished deck, then clicks through to a LinkedIn headline that says something different, then opens a portfolio with different featured projects, forms an impression of inconsistency at the exact moment of highest signal density. The polish amplifies the incoherence rather than concealing it — a better-designed surface makes the contradiction more visible.

Option B (coherence audit) ensures that the ten-minute presentation functions as the chapter intends: "a reader who reads all three artifacts in sequence should encounter one person, not three slightly different people." The chapter's specific checklist for the audit: positioning sentence matches LinkedIn headline; same archetype legible in all three; same three to five projects featured across all surfaces; voice consistent when read aloud. Ten days is sufficient to run this audit and revise the two or three surfaces that have drifted. The return — a presentation where every surface confirms the same argument — satisfies the Airbnb standard directly.
