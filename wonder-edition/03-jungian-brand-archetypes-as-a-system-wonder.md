## The Strange Question

In January 2009, Arnell Group — one of the most respected design firms in the United States — redesigned the Tropicana Pure Premium carton. Design critics praised the new packaging as cleaner, more contemporary, more sophisticated. Tropicana's leadership approved it. The rollout was well-funded and carefully planned.

Within two months, Tropicana lost more than $30 million in sales. The company reversed the redesign.

The new packaging was not ugly. It was not misleading. It did not violate any regulations. So why did customers who had bought Tropicana for twenty years walk past the new carton without picking it up?

---

## First Intuition

The intuitive answer is that customers dislike change. They had a habit. The old packaging triggered the habit. The new packaging did not. This intuition comes from daily experience: people reorder the same products, follow familiar routes, and resist interruptions to established routines. Change feels bad; consistency feels good.

Under this model, the solution is gradual transition: introduce changes slowly so customers can adapt without noticing. Keep some elements, change others. Give people time to update their mental model.

> **► Planning prompt:** Before reading further, state your explanation for why customers rejected the Tropicana redesign. What prior experience are you drawing on? If you were advising Tropicana before the launch, what specific change would you have warned them not to make? Write your prediction before continuing.

---

## The Surprise

But the rejection was not gradual — it was immediate. Some customers bought competitor products thinking they were buying Tropicana. They did not experience confusion and then adjustment. They experienced no recognition at all. The Tropicana brand they knew had, from their perspective, simply not been present on the shelf that morning.

Gap's 2010 redesign produced the same pattern. Within 24 hours of the new logo's release — before most customers had even seen the new packaging in stores — the backlash was large enough to trend nationally. Six days later, the company reversed course. Twenty years of recognition did not adapt or migrate; it evaporated against a new visual that could not be connected to the model customers had built.

The "change is uncomfortable" model predicts slow adjustment. These cases show near-instantaneous rejection.

> **► Monitoring prompt:** In your own words, describe what your initial prediction assumed about how customer brand recognition works. What specific part does the speed of these rejections contradict? What does your current model still fail to explain about why a design that critics praised produced customer rejection within hours?

## The Hidden Structure

Therefore, brand recognition does not work the way change-aversion intuition suggests. The mechanism is recognition handles: specific visual, verbal, or product cues that customers have learned, over many exposures, to associate with a brand's identity. The orange-with-straw on Tropicana was not a decoration. It was the pattern customers trained their eyes to scan for in the juice aisle. When that pattern disappeared, the brand disappeared with it.

The Mark/Pearson archetype system explains why specific recognition handles form, persist, and break. An archetype is a consistency-enforcement device — the constraint that makes every brand decision point toward the same identity. A Sage brand produces authoritative, plainspoken communication at every touchpoint. An Innocent brand produces simple, wholesome, unremarkably positive communication at every touchpoint. Customers build their recognition model not from one interaction but from hundreds. The archetype is what makes those hundreds of interactions point at the same thing.

It is tempting to think that archetype selection is a brand styling decision — like choosing colors or fonts — that can be revised when the market shifts or competitors change. But the three cases in this chapter show the opposite. The correct model holds that an archetype is a strategic commitment made in the customer's brain, not in the brand's documents. The key distinction is between the archetype as an internal declaration and the archetype as an accumulated recognition asset in customer memory: what the brand decides internally can change instantly; what customers have learned cannot.

The shadow — the form the archetype takes when its commitments curdle — follows the same structural logic. Every archetype's strength is the source of its shadow. The Innocent's strength is simplicity and purity. The shadow of purity is denial: an Innocent brand under pressure starts refusing to acknowledge complexity. The Sage's strength is expertise and truth. The shadow of expertise is dogmatism: a Sage brand under pressure becomes certain, preachy, and unable to update. The shadow does not appear from outside — it grows from the inside of the archetype's own commitments.

## Try Looking At It This Way

**Target:** Brand archetypes as accumulated recognition assets — commitments that become load-bearing precisely because customers build cognitive models around them.

**Base:** A recurring musical theme — a leitmotif — in a film score.

**Features:**
- The musical theme corresponds to the brand archetype — both are patterns that recur across a complex work to signal a specific character or identity.
- Repetition across many scenes corresponds to repeated brand touchpoints — both accumulate meaning through exposure, not through any single instance.
- A scene where the theme plays out of context corresponds to archetype drift — both create cognitive dissonance when the signal contradicts the expected character.

**Commonalities:** In John Williams's score for Star Wars, Darth Vader's theme (the Imperial March) builds its meaning through repetition. The first time a viewer hears it, it is a pattern. The fifth time, it signals menace. The twentieth time, hearing it over an unrelated scene creates immediate dissonance — something important has changed. The audience does not consult a program note to know this; they feel the contradiction immediately. Brand customers build the same pattern-to-meaning relationship across brand touchpoints. The Tropicana customer who walked past the new carton was experiencing the same dissonance — an expected pattern replaced by one that could not be mapped to the existing model.

**Boundaries:** A film score is authored by one person for one work, and leitmotifs are deliberately placed. Brand touchpoints are produced by many different people across many different contexts, and consistency requires active enforcement rather than authorial control. The archetype in a brand is more fragile than a leitmotif because it must survive handoffs between designers, writers, engineers, and marketers who may not share a common vocabulary for the brand's identity.

**Conclusions:** Recognition builds through consistency across repeated exposures, not through the quality of any individual touchpoint. An archetype's value is not what it says but what it enforces across every decision point that produces a customer-facing artifact.

---

## Where The Analogy Breaks

Unlike a film score, a brand's identity must remain consistent over years and across thousands of independent decision-makers. This matters because a film leitmotif can be recalled perfectly and replicated exactly. A brand archetype must be interpreted in each new context — a new product launch, a new market, a new crisis — and applied to situations the original architects never anticipated. No archetype document can pre-specify every application. The brand professionals who make daily decisions must internalize the archetype well enough to apply it correctly in situations where no rule exists. That is not score-reading — it is judgment under uncertainty, and it is the reason archetype drift happens slowly rather than all at once.

## Small Discovery

**Raw data:** A bakery chain operates 40 locations in one city. It started as a neighborhood shop known for simple, unpretentious baked goods — whole-grain loaves, plain croissants, no specialty coffee drinks, no seasonal menus. Over three years, under pressure from artisanal competitors, the chain gradually introduced seasonal offerings, specialty lattes, elaborate pastries with foreign names, and a new packaging design using matte black and gold.

A market research firm surveyed customers who had visited at least five times before the changes and tracked their return visit rates:

| Year | Returning customers (5+ prior visits) who returned within 60 days | New customers acquired |
|------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------|
| Year 1 (before changes) | 74% | 1,200 |
| Year 2 (early changes) | 71% | 1,800 |
| Year 3 (full transformation) | 44% | 2,400 |

**Pattern search:** New customer acquisition increased each year. Returning customer retention fell sharply in Year 3. What does this pattern suggest about who the new brand attracted — and who it lost?

**Prediction:** In Year 4, if the chain reverses the changes and returns to its original identity, would returning customer retention recover to Year 1 levels? Or would something else happen? Write your prediction before continuing.

---

The Year 3 pattern shows the chain successfully attracting a new audience while losing the audience that built its business. New customers respond to the artisanal positioning. Returning customers who built their recognition model on "unpretentious neighborhood bakery" find the new brand unrecognizable as the place they chose repeatedly.

If the chain reverses in Year 4, retention from original customers may partially recover — but the archetype transition has already introduced a competing model in new customer minds. The chain now has two recognition assets in conflict: the memory of the original brand in returning customers and the expectation of artisanal sophistication in new ones. This is the Mark/Pearson archetype drift problem made visible in customer behavior data. Recognition assets do not simply reset when the brand reverses course — they have to be rebuilt against the competing model that grew up during the drift period.

## What This Changes

A student who has internalized this chapter can now answer a question that would have confused them before: why does a brand redesign praised by design critics fail in the market? The answer is not that critics are wrong — it is that design quality and recognition value are different properties. A recognition handle is valuable not because it is aesthetically excellent but because customers learned to scan for it.

What looks different now in a real brand decision: a product team proposing a visual refresh must now ask not "is the new design better?" but "does the new design retain the recognition handles that customers' models depend on?" Those are different evaluations requiring different evidence.

**Practice Bridge:** When writing the Product Requirements Document in Chapter 4, a student can now specify not just what the tool does but what it will consistently signal in every touchpoint — the tagline, the error messages, the README, the onboarding sequence. Each of those is an archetype decision before it is a design decision. Naming the archetype first makes those decisions consistent by default.

What this chapter leaves open: how brands successfully evolve their archetype without triggering the rejection mechanism. Apple transitioned from Outlaw to Magician over a decade without losing its audience. The chapter acknowledges this as unresolved — the mechanism is not cleanly specified, and copying Apple's trajectory without understanding it is likely to reproduce its costs rather than its outcomes. Chapter 8 addresses brand strategy with a full brand development sequence.

## Wonder Questions

What specific difference between the three cases explains the different speeds of rejection — and what does that difference reveal about which part of the archetype's recognition asset was violated in each case?

2. The chapter says that reading what a brand refuses to do is as informative as reading what it does. If you were trying to identify a brand's archetype from only its negative space — its absences, refusals, and exclusions — what would you look for in Nike's public record?

3. The Sage's shadow is dogmatism. A brand could avoid the shadow by deliberately maintaining uncertainty and acknowledging limitations. But a Sage brand's value proposition depends on expertise and authority. How do you maintain the Sage's strength while avoiding the shadow — and what observable behavior distinguishes a Sage brand managing its shadow from a Sage brand activating it?

4. If a brand picks an archetype that is authentic to its founder but incompatible with its target audience, should it change the archetype or the audience? What evidence would you need before deciding?

5. The chapter's three cases (Tropicana, Gap, New Coke) all involve established brands drifting from their original archetype. Can a brand fail by committing to an archetype too rigidly — refusing any adaptation even as market conditions change? What would that failure look like?

---

> **What the concept is:** A brand archetype is a consistency-enforcement device — the constraint that makes every downstream brand decision coherent and that accumulates, touchpoint by touchpoint, into the recognition asset customers use to find and identify a brand without conscious deliberation.
>
> **What it explains:** Why technically well-executed redesigns produce customer rejection; why archetype drift triggers immediate recognition failure rather than gradual adjustment; why the shadow — the failure mode structurally related to each archetype's strength — is the most predictable form of brand breakdown.
>
> **What it does NOT mean:** It does not mean brands cannot change or evolve. The chapter documents successful archetype transitions but acknowledges the mechanism is not fully understood. What it does mean is that change must preserve the recognition handles that constitute the accumulated asset — and that changes driven by competitive pressure to adopt a competitor's archetype almost always fail, because the challenger's archetype works for the challenger's position, not the incumbent's.
>
> **What comes next:** Chapter 4's Product Requirements Document is the first place the committed archetype becomes a product constraint — determining what the tool includes, what it excludes, and how it is named and described. The archetype goes dormant as an explicit topic and becomes a silent decision filter.
