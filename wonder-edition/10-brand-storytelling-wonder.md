## The Strange Question

In April 2017, Pepsi released a commercial featuring model Kendall Jenner at a protest-style demonstration. Jenner hands a police officer a can of Pepsi. The officer smiles. Tension resolves.

The commercial was pulled within 24 hours after intense public criticism.

Pepsi's advertising agency spent real money and significant creative talent on this commercial. It was not carelessly made. The production quality was high. The intent — to associate Pepsi with unity and social connection — was coherent with Pepsi's historical positioning as a brand of shared joy.

So why did a well-funded, professionally executed commercial fail so completely that the brand had to apologize within a day?

---

## First Intuition

The intuitive answer is that Pepsi misjudged the social climate — the commercial was tone-deaf to the political moment, reducing complex social protest to a refreshment moment. This comes from the common analysis of brand failures: they misread cultural context, they should have consulted more diverse voices in production, they should have tested the concept more carefully.

Under this model, the solution is better research and more inclusive creative development. Understand the cultural moment better, and the commercial would have worked.

Is this primarily a cultural misjudgment, a production quality issue, a timing problem, or something else? What specific change would have made the commercial succeed? Write your prediction before continuing.

## The Surprise

But the chapter's analysis reveals the failure operated below the level of cultural timing — it was structural. Pepsi's archetype is broadly Innocent (joy, refreshment, simple pleasure) with Jester elements. Protest and social-justice themes belong to the Outlaw archetype — the energy of the Outlaw comes from naming something the establishment does and insisting there is another way. The Innocent's promise is "the world is good and our product reflects that goodness." Taking Outlaw content (protest, systemic tension, police confrontation) and resolving it with an Innocent gesture (a can of soda, a smile) does not just misjudge the cultural moment — it inverts the meaning of the protest itself.

The failure operated through what the chapter calls Pathway 3: the story's cultural content belongs to an archetype the brand does not inhabit. An Innocent brand entering social-movement content is borrowing content it has no claim to. The audience perceives the gap between the content's weight and the brand's capacity to hold it — not as a cultural misjudgment but as a kind of falseness that is felt before it is articulated.

Better cultural research would have found advisors who flagged the dissonance. But the structural problem — an Innocent archetype attempting an Outlaw's story content — would have remained. The fix was not better cultural sensitivity; it was a different story shape entirely, native to the Innocent's actual archetype.

## The Hidden Structure

Therefore, story shapes have archetypal commitments built into them. Choosing a shape that does not fit the brand's archetype produces narratives that audiences experience as false — even when every individual element is well-executed, even when the cultural sensitivity is genuine, even when the intent is positive.

The chapter introduces three layered storytelling frameworks: Campbell's Hero's Journey (the universal story architecture — departure, initiation, return), Booker's Seven Basic Plots (the taxonomy of plot shapes available — Overcoming the Monster, Rags to Riches, the Quest, and four others), and Miller's Customer-as-Hero (the brand-marketing application: the customer is always the hero, the brand is always the guide). These three operate at different levels: Campbell provides the deep architecture, Booker provides the shape categories, Miller provides the positioning discipline.

It is tempting to think that the key insight is Miller's positioning rule — the customer is the hero, not the brand. That insight is real and important. But the chapter's deeper mechanism is the archetype-shape fit: a Hero archetype can tell a Hero's Journey directly; a Sage archetype tells Methodology and Quest stories; an Outlaw tells Rebellion stories; an Innocent cannot hold conflict, shadow, and systemic critique without abandoning the commitments that make the Innocent brand recognizable. The correct model holds that the archetype is the filter on which story shapes are available. The key distinction is between choosing a story because it matches the cultural moment and choosing a story because it matches the archetype — only the second produces sustainable brand storytelling.

The three failure pathways make this concrete: the story shape may require emotional registers the archetype cannot deliver (Pathway 1), it may position the brand in a role inconsistent with its archetype (Pathway 2), or the story's cultural content may belong to a different archetype entirely (Pathway 3). All three were active in the Pepsi commercial simultaneously.

## Try Looking At It This Way

**Target:** Archetype-story alignment — the principle that story shapes have archetypal commitments that determine whether a brand can hold specific narrative content authentically.

**Base:** Actor casting in film.

**Features:**
- The brand's archetype corresponds to the actor's established screen persona — both set audience expectations about what the performer can authentically deliver.
- Archetype-native story shapes correspond to roles that fit the actor's established range — both produce performance coherence.
- Archetype-incompatible story shapes correspond to casting against type — both risk producing performances the audience experiences as false.

**Commonalities:** A film audience brings expectations shaped by the actor's prior work. An actor established in comedic roles who is cast as a grief-stricken dramatic lead may give a technically proficient performance that still reads as wrong — not because the performance is bad, but because the audience's prior model of the actor cannot hold the new character type. Brand storytelling operates the same mechanism: a brand established in joy and refreshment (Innocent archetype) attempting grief-and-resistance story content (Outlaw archetype) may execute the commercial flawlessly while still producing a performance the audience cannot accept. The expectation model the audience built over decades cannot hold the new story shape.

**Boundaries:** A film actor can choose to deliberately build a new persona through a sustained sequence of role choices over years — career strategy can shift type. A brand archetype cannot be changed as quickly as an actor's next project, because the recognition asset is accumulated in the audience's memory, not in the brand's self-conception. A brand that attempts an archetype transition must maintain the new archetype consistently across all touchpoints for long enough to rebuild the audience's model — which takes years, not a single campaign.

**Conclusions:** Archetype-story fit is the constraint that determines whether a brand can hold specific narrative content authentically. The content's quality is irrelevant if the archetype cannot deliver the emotional register the story requires.

---

## Where The Analogy Breaks

Unlike film casting, a brand cannot reshoot scenes that do not work. When a commercial airs, it is already in the market, building or damaging the brand's model in the audience's memory. An actor in a poor performance can recover with the next role; a brand that publishes an archetype-incompatible story must issue a public retraction and rebuild the damaged model. The cost of archetype-story mismatch in branding is more permanent than in film because the brand's touchpoints are cumulative — each one adds to the model — while film performances are evaluated individually.

## Small Discovery

**Raw data:** A consumer research firm presented five different advertisements for the same product (a home security system) to different audience panels. Each advertisement used a different narrative structure. The firm measured purchase intent and "brand fit" — whether the ad felt authentic to the brand's established identity.

| Ad version | Narrative structure | Brand fit score (1–10) | Purchase intent (1–10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Fear and threat narrative (Overcoming the Monster shape) | 8.4 | 7.1 |
| B | Family warmth and protection (Caregiver/Service shape) | 8.7 | 7.6 |
| C | Tech innovation and mastery (Hero's Journey / capability shape) | 5.2 | 5.8 |
| D | Social proof and belonging (Everyman shape) | 6.8 | 7.2 |
| E | Rebellion against surveillance capitalism (Outlaw/Rebellion shape) | 3.1 | 4.4 |

The home security brand had been established as a Caregiver archetype through years of "protect what matters most" messaging.

**Pattern search:** Ad E has the lowest brand fit and the lowest purchase intent despite being, arguably, the most culturally resonant framing for technology in 2024. What does this reveal about the relationship between cultural resonance and archetype fit?

**Prediction:** If the brand ran Ad A as a campaign and over three years transitioned its messaging toward the Rebellion narrative in Ad E, what would happen to brand fit scores over the transition period? Write your prediction before continuing.

---

Ad E's failure to produce purchase intent despite cultural resonance confirms the chapter's mechanism: cultural resonance is necessary but not sufficient for advertising effectiveness. The Rebellion narrative is culturally available and timely — the concerns about surveillance technology are real and widely felt. But the Caregiver brand cannot hold that content without abandoning the commitments that make it a Caregiver. The audience's model of "protect what matters most" cannot be reconciled with "resist surveillance capitalism" — the first is about protection, the second about disruption.

A gradual transition from Ad A toward Ad E over three years would show declining brand fit during the transition — the audience would experience the brand as incoherent, caught between its established Caregiver commitments and emerging Outlaw content. Purchase intent would likely decline with brand fit. The transition would only "work" (restore brand fit scores) if it was sustained long enough to rebuild the audience's model around the new archetype — which requires consistency across all brand touchpoints, not just advertising. This is the Apple transition mechanism the chapter references: Outlaw to Magician over a decade, gradual enough that the audience's model updated rather than broke.

## What This Changes

A student who has internalized this chapter can now answer a question that would have confused them before: why did the Pepsi Kendall Jenner commercial fail despite high production quality and cultural intent? The answer is not cultural insensitivity alone — it is structural archetype-story mismatch. The Innocent archetype cannot hold Outlaw story content without inverting the meaning of both.

What looks different now in a real content decision: before publishing a case study, thought-leadership piece, or campaign, the diagnostic question is "does this story shape fit my archetype, or am I borrowing content from a different archetype's territory?" That question catches misfires before they are in the market.

**Practice Bridge:** When writing the customer-as-hero case study required in this chapter, a student must specify: the story's emotional arc (which of the three failure pathways would this story risk?), the customer's role (hero), the brand's role (guide), and the archetype's native story shape (which of Booker's seven plots most naturally fits the archetype). A case study that cannot specify all four is not yet architected well enough to write.

What this chapter leaves open: how to tell compelling stories within the constraint of the archetype, when the archetype's native story shapes feel less culturally urgent than shapes belonging to other archetypes. The chapter establishes that archetype-inconsistent stories misfire; it does not provide an exhaustive taxonomy of what compelling Sage stories or compelling Innocent stories look like in contemporary cultural contexts. Chapter 11's portfolio construction is where the storytelling frameworks get applied to specific artifacts the student has built.

> **What the concept is:** Brand storytelling is the discipline of selecting story shapes that match the brand's archetype — because story shapes have archetypal commitments, and choosing a shape that belongs to a different archetype produces narratives that audiences experience as false, regardless of execution quality.
>
> **What it explains:** Why the Pepsi Kendall Jenner commercial failed despite professional execution; why some brand campaigns feel authentic while others feel like borrowing; why the customer-as-hero framing is insufficient on its own without archetype-shape alignment.
>
> **What it does NOT mean:** It does not mean brands should only tell stories that are politically and culturally safe. It means that culturally charged story content belongs to specific archetypes, and a brand that enters that content must be operating from an archetype that can hold it — or the audience will feel the falseness before they can articulate the structural reason.
>
